\chapter{Data}
\label{ch:data}
% =============================================================================

This chapter describes how the firm-year panel is built. The dataset
comes entirely from primary sources: no commercial research database
(such as Compustat, CRSP, or a vendor-supplied 10-K text corpus) is
used. Financial variables come directly from the SEC EDGAR Company
Facts API, narrative sections are parsed from the original HTML of
the filings, and return-based measures are computed from raw daily
prices. This has two advantages. First, every transformation of the
data is documented; there are no undocumented vendor adjustments
between the filing and the regression input. Second, the textual and
financial data can be matched on the same filing, fiscal period, and
reporting entity, which matters when the research question concerns
the information in a specific document. The cost is that several
data problems that commercial providers usually handle (XBRL tag
heterogeneity, inconsistent HTML structure, amendments) have to be
dealt with directly. Much of this chapter describes how these
problems were solved and how the choices were validated.

Figure~\ref{fig:data_pipeline} gives an overview of the
data-preparation process. The pipeline starts from the raw SEC sources,
applies the sample filters, and then runs two parallel branches---a
financial (XBRL) branch and a textual (10-K narrative) branch---that
are merged into the final regression-ready firm-year panel.

\section{Selecting the Sample}
\label{sec:sample_selection}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The starting universe is the set of the 1{,}000 largest U.S.\ public
companies by market capitalisation, identified from the SEC EDGAR
company-tickers file, which maps each ticker to its Central Index Key
(CIK) and exchange listing.  Three filters are then applied in
sequence.  First, we keep only entities that the SEC classifies as
``operating'' companies.  This step removes exchange-traded funds,
closed-end funds, blank-cheque (SPAC) vehicles, and shell entities,
none of which produce the kind of narrative risk-factor disclosure
that the thesis studies, and whose financial statements are not
comparable to those of operating businesses.  Second, we exclude
firms with SIC codes in the range 6000--6999 (finance, insurance, and
real estate), as is standard: financial firms face different
regulation, their balance sheets consist mostly of financial assets
and liabilities, and ratios such as leverage or operating cash flow
over assets mean something different for them. Third, we require at
least five fiscal years of readable XBRL data from 10-K filings, and
we keep a firm only if at least 90\% of its filings have a valid
365-day post-filing volatility. A filing has a valid volatility
when the stock has at least 200 daily prices in the year after the
filing. The five-year minimum gives enough within-firm variation for
fixed-effects estimation. The return rule removes firms with long
trading gaps. Because this rule looks at prices \emph{after} each
filing, it also removes some firms that were delisted during the
sample; together with the fact that the starting list contains
firms that exist today, this means the sample favours surviving
firms (see the limitations in Section~\ref{sec:estimation}).

\paragraph{Sample size.}
We focus on large, liquid firms rather than the full EDGAR universe
for several reasons. First, the regressions use firm fixed effects
with standard errors clustered by firm and fiscal year. As \textcite{petersen2009}
emphasises, the reliability of clustered inference depends mainly on
the number of clusters, not the number of observations. With
526~firm clusters, the cross-section is large enough for
cluster-robust inference. Second, large firms have more complete
machine-readable filing histories, which means fewer missing
observations and less measurement error in the text pipeline. Third,
smaller firms are more likely to be illiquid, thinly traded, or to
have poorly formatted filings, which adds noise to both return-based
and accounting variables. Finally, a large-firm sample keeps the text
processing manageable and gives a relatively homogeneous set of
issuers.

The sample size is in line with prior work on the text and
financials of annual filings. Intermediate stages of the pipeline
contain between 539 and 548 firms; the 30-day regression sample
contains 526 firms.
\textcite{campbell2014} study about 9{,}000 firm-years of risk-factor
disclosures from large U.S.\ firms; \textcite{loughran2011} use a
broader set of roughly 50{,}000 firm-years, but their goal---validating
a sentiment dictionary---calls for maximal cross-sectional coverage,
whereas the joint volume--specificity test here uses
within-industry-year variation across about 526 firms.

\section{Choosing the Filing Frequency --- why annual 10-Ks?}
\label{sec:annual_vs_quarterly}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

We use annual 10-K filings rather than quarterly 10-Q reports.
Quarterly data would roughly triple the sample, but two practical
problems, shown in Tables~\ref{tab:q4_reliability}--\ref{tab:text_availability},
rule this out.

\paragraph{Financial data gaps in Q4.}
10-Q filings cover only Q1--Q3, so any quarterly panel must reconstruct
Q4 as the difference between the annual figure and the sum of the three
preceding quarters.  Table~\ref{tab:q4_reliability} shows that this
residual approach is not usable for operating cash flow: the three
quarterly facts coexist in only $7\%$ of firm-years, because most firms
report cumulative year-to-date cash flows rather than standalone
quarterly amounts.  Operating cash flow is the primary input into our
OCF-to-assets ratio, so a quarterly panel would lose the cash-flow-based
robustness check at the source.  Even for net income and operating
income, where coverage exceeds $80\%$, the implied Q4 disagrees with the
explicit Q4 by more than one percent of the annual total in about
$10$--$14\%$ of comparable firm-years.

The residual and the reported fourth quarter differ because they
come from documents prepared at different times and with different
scrutiny. Interim 10-Q figures are reviewed but not audited. The
10-K is audited, and year-end audit adjustments (impairments, tax
true-ups, changes in estimates, reclassifications to discontinued
operations) are usually booked in the fourth quarter without
restating earlier quarters. The residual Q4 absorbs these
adjustments, whereas an explicit Q4 disclosed in the 10-K (for
example in a selected-quarterly-data note) may have been recast on a
comparable basis. The explicit Q4 value in the Company Facts API also
often comes from a \emph{later} filing that shows the prior period as
a restated comparative, so the two values can reflect different
versions of the same accounting history. 52/53-week fiscal calendars
and rounding or scaling differences add small mechanical gaps. None
of these is a data error as such, but a reconstructed quarterly
panel would mix audited and unaudited, restated and original figures
in ways that are hard to control.

\input{tables/q4_reliability}

\paragraph{Missing risk-factor text in 10-Q.}
SEC Regulation S-K only requires firms to update risk factors in
quarterly filings when ``material changes'' have occurred since the
last 10-K.  In practice, only about half the 10-Q filings in our pilot
sample (15~firms, 90~filings) contained an actual risk-factor section.
The rest only referred back to the annual report
(Table~\ref{tab:text_availability}). The year-on-year text variables
in our specifications therefore cannot be built at a quarterly
frequency.

\input{tables/text_availability}

\paragraph{Chosen frequency.}
Tables~\ref{tab:q4_reliability} and~\ref{tab:text_availability}
favour an annual design. A quarterly panel would require rebuilding
fourth-quarter accounting variables from incomplete and often
inconsistent XBRL data, and would rely on risk-factor sections that
are often missing from 10-Q filings because firms need only report
material changes since the last annual report. Both problems reduce
data availability and add measurement error. Annual 10-K filings
contain full fiscal-year accounting data and complete MD\&A and
risk-factor sections, so all explanatory variables can be measured
the same way across firms and years.

The annual frequency also matches the disclosure process. The text
measures in this thesis describe firms' full annual filings, not
quarterly updates. Matching the observation interval to the
reporting frequency of the documents avoids imputation and
artificial within-year variation, and changes in the text measures
then correspond to actual revisions of the annual disclosure.

An annual firm-year panel is also the convention in the 10-K
textual-analysis literature.
\textcite{li2008} analyses 55{,}719 firm-year observations
constructed from annual 10-K filings, while \textcite{loughran2011}
develop and validate financial text dictionaries using a sample of
50{,}115 firm-year 10-K filings.  Both studies treat the annual
filing as the unit of analysis. The XBRL coverage comparison
(Table~\ref{tab:xbrl_coverage}) and a consolidated summary
(Table~\ref{tab:annual_vs_quarterly}) are in
Appendix~\ref{app:diagnostics}.

\section{XBRL Tag Consistency Dilemma}
\label{sec:xbrl_tags}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

In the SEC EDGAR Company Facts API, one accounting concept can be
reported under several US-GAAP taxonomy elements. Filers have some
discretion in choosing concepts, and the taxonomy changes over time.
Prior XBRL research shows that the same accounting variable may map
to several taxonomy concepts, so these must be mapped explicitly
before analysis \parencite{chychyla2015}.  For
example, operating cash flow may be reported as either
\texttt{NetCashProvided\-ByUsed\-InOperating\-Activities} or
\texttt{NetCashProvided\-ByUsed\-InOperating\-Activities\-Continuing\-Operations};
net income may be reported as \texttt{NetIncomeLoss},
\texttt{ProfitLoss}, or
\texttt{IncomeLossFrom\-ContinuingOperations}; and stockholders'
equity may be presented either including or excluding
non-controlling interests (NCI). These tags often overlap but are
not always identical. Treating all of them as interchangeable would
add measurement noise, while choosing one tag for all firms would
drop observations for firms that consistently use another valid
element.

We handle this problem with a two-part \emph{tag-harmonisation} process:
(i)~\emph{equivalence groups} for tags that are economically
interchangeable, and (ii)~\emph{per-firm tag locking} for tags that
are not.

\paragraph{Equivalence groups.}
Following the concept-mapping approach of \textcite{chychyla2015}, we
define equivalence groups for tags that differ in label or taxonomy
representation but not in economic content.
\texttt{NetCashProvided\-ByUsed\-InOperating\-Activities} and
\texttt{NetCashProvided\-ByUsed\-InOperating\-Activities\-Continuing\-Operations}
are treated as equivalent operating-cash-flow measures, while
\texttt{NetIncomeLoss} and \texttt{ProfitLoss} are treated as
interchangeable net-income measures.  By contrast,
\texttt{IncomeLossFrom\-ContinuingOperations} is excluded from the
equivalence set because it excludes discontinued operations and so
measures something different.

We also check these groupings in the data. In the Company Facts API
there are 6{,}113 firm-years in which both members of an equivalence
pair are reported. The differences between paired tags are
negligible, with a median ROA bias of 0.033~percentage points
(Appendix~\ref{app:variables}, Table~\ref{tab:tag_provenance}), so
we treat the paired tags as two representations of the same
quantity. In the few cases where both tags are present and differ
materially, the parent-company value is used.

\paragraph{Per-firm tag locking.}
Where alternative tags measure different things, such as
stockholders' equity with versus without NCI, or
\texttt{Income\-Loss\-From\-Continuing\-Operations} versus total net
income, mixing tags within a firm's time series would create
measurement error. We assign each firm the tag it uses most often
over its reporting history and set values reported under other tags
to missing.

\paragraph{Missing balance-sheet values.}
If total liabilities are missing, we compute them as total assets
minus stockholders' equity. If total assets or total liabilities
are still missing, we copy the value from the firm's previous
fiscal year, but only when that year is directly before. We never
fill a value from a \emph{later} year, because a later balance
sheet was not public at the time of the filing.

With both procedures, the complete-case rate for the five regression
controls rises from 78.9\% to 92.7\% of firm-years. A small
inconsistency remains: about 4\% of firms use a
continuing-operations operating-cash-flow tag while reporting total
net income. Because these mismatches are small, we keep the
observations and rely on winsorisation to limit their influence.
Such researcher-imposed harmonisation is common in XBRL-based panel
work \parencite{chychyla2015}.

Per-firm tag choices and the overlap diagnostic are reported in
Appendix~\ref{app:variables}
(Table~\ref{tab:tag_provenance}).

\section{How do we extract text from 10-K filings?}
\label{sec:text_extraction}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The study uses two sections of the 10-K: Item~1A \emph{Risk Factors}
and Item~7 \emph{Management's Discussion and Analysis of Financial
Condition and Results of Operations} (MD\&A). This section describes
how we locate and extract them from the raw HTML filings on SEC
EDGAR.

\subsection{Source of textual data}
\label{ssec:text_source}

All filings are retrieved directly from SEC EDGAR, the Commission's
public repository of corporate disclosures, without any intermediate
vendor or pre-processed corpus.  Retrieval proceeds in two steps.
First, the EDGAR Submissions API\footnote{%
\texttt{https://data.sec.gov/submissions/CIK\{cik\}.json}; the
endpoint family is documented at
\url{https://www.sec.gov/search-filings/edgar-application-programming-interfaces}.
The same endpoint family provides the Company Facts data used for the
financial branch (Section~\ref{sec:xbrl_tags}).} is queried for each
company's Central Index Key (CIK).  The response lists every filing
the company has ever submitted together with its form type, filing
date, accession number, and the name of the \emph{primary document},
i.e.\ the main HTML file of the filing as opposed to its exhibits.
Second, for each original 10-K the primary document is downloaded
from the EDGAR archive at
\texttt{sec.gov/Archives/edgar/data/\{CIK\}/\{accession\}/\{document\}}.
Where the filing index indicates that narrative content has been
placed in an exhibit (Section~\ref{ssec:section_id}), the exhibit
file is fetched from the same accession folder.  All requests carry
the declared user-agent header that the SEC requires and are
throttled to stay below the published rate limit of ten requests per
second. The download can be reproduced from the scripts in the
accompanying repository.

From the panel of about 6{,}700 firm-year observations, we drop
10-K/A amendments.  These typically consist of a cover page and a
restated exhibit and do not contain the full narrative sections of
the original 10-K. Financial data for amended fiscal years are kept,
since the XBRL pipeline already picks up amended figures when
available (Section~\ref{sec:xbrl_tags}). Text is extracted only from
original 10-K filings.

\subsection{Section identification and exhibit fallback}
\label{ssec:section_id}

Unlike structured XBRL data, the textual parts of 10-K filings are
buried inside HTML with no standardised tagging, mixed with
table-of-contents (TOC) entries, running headers, and inline
cross-references.  A naive search for the string ``Item~1A'' would
therefore typically land on the TOC entry or on a cross-reference
such as ``see Item~1A'' rather than on the section heading.
We use a four-stage HTML parser that operates on
the document structure \emph{before} the text is flattened:

\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Candidate detection.}  The parser scans the HTML tree
    for elements whose text matches a regular expression for the
    target ``Item~X'' strings (``Item~1A'', ``Item~7'', and the
    headers that delimit them).  A match is kept as a candidate
    header only if it also carries at least one formatting cue
    typical of a section heading: bold font weight, ALL-CAPS text, or
    a font size of at least 12\,pt.  Two classes of false positives
    are rejected outright: elements that are hyperlinks (navigation
    links in the TOC) and elements that are surrounded by other
    ``Item'' references within a window of 300~characters, which is
    the characteristic signature of a table of contents.
  \item \textbf{In-place tagging.}  Each confirmed candidate is
    marked with a unique sentinel string inside the HTML tree.  Only
    then is the document converted to plain text.  Tagging before
    flattening preserves the position of the header exactly, whereas
    locating headers in already-flattened text would lose the
    formatting information used in step~1.
  \item \textbf{Candidate selection.}  When several candidates for
    the same Item survive (for instance a TOC entry that happened to
    be bold), the parser selects the one followed by the longest
    stretch of text before the next expected Item header.  The true
    section is, by construction, the one with substantive content
    after it.
  \item \textbf{Section bounding.}  The section ends at the next
    Item header in filing order: Item~1B, 1C, or 2 for Item~1A, and
    Item~7A or 8 for Item~7.  Accepting several terminators handles
    filings that omit optional items
    (Item~1B is not required for smaller filers; Item~1C was only
    introduced for fiscal years ending after December~2023).
\end{enumerate}

\paragraph{Exhibit fallback.}
Some filers, especially in the early part of the sample, incorporate
the narrative sections by reference to the annual report to
shareholders, which is attached as Exhibit~13.  In such cases the
main document contains only a short pointer (``The information
required by this Item is incorporated by reference to \ldots'').
If the recovered section is shorter than 200~words, the parser treats
it as a stub, follows the reference to the exhibit file listed in the
filing index, and reruns the same four-stage procedure on the exhibit
using title-only patterns (``Risk Factors'', ``Management's
Discussion and Analysis''), since exhibits do not carry Item
numbering.

\subsection{Post-Processing}
\label{ssec:text_postprocessing}

After HTML-to-text conversion the parser strips three layout
artefacts: ``\textit{$\langle$page number$\rangle$~Table
of Contents}'' headers, standalone page numbers on
lines that do not contain any other text, and non-breaking-space characters. The cleaning
rules target only patterns that cannot be part of meaningful text
and are applied after section boundaries have already been set.

\subsection{Coverage and Limitations}
\label{ssec:text_coverage}

The extraction pipeline was validated on two samples: the
50~most recent filings (fiscal year 2024, both sections recovered
in all 50) and a stratified random sample of 100~filings covering
2010--2024 (97/100 successful, with three failures driven by
PDF-only exhibits or the absence of any formatting cue for the
section header). On the full panel we expect a coverage rate of
roughly 97\%, with missing values treated as missing in the
textual variable construction.

\paragraph{LLM-assisted quality audit.}
To check that the parser finds and bounds the right sections, we
had an independent reviewer assess a stratified random sample of
filings. Independent annotation is a standard way to assess
automated text processing; \textcite{benoit2016} show that reliable
aggregate assessments can be obtained even when individual judgments
are imperfect. Large language models (LLMs) perform structured
annotation tasks about as well as, and sometimes better than, human
annotators, and do so consistently \parencite{gilardi2023}. The LLM
was used only as a reviewer of the output; all section boundaries
come from the deterministic pipeline described above.

A stratified random sample of 100~filings was drawn, with 20~filings
selected from each five-year period between 2010 and 2024.  Both
Item~1A (Risk Factors) and Item~7 (MD\&A) were evaluated for every
filing, yielding 200~section-level assessments.  This sample size
implies Wilson 95\,\% confidence intervals of approximately
$\pm 3$~percentage points for proportions near 95\,\%
\parencite{agresti1998}.

For each extracted section, Google Gemini~2.5~Flash evaluated five
criteria: (i)~correct section identity, (ii)~start-boundary
accuracy, (iii)~end-boundary accuracy, (iv)~content quality, and
(v)~completeness.  Each criterion was rated as Pass, Minor issue, or
Fail.  Detailed results are reported in Appendix~\ref{app:variables}
(Table~\ref{tab:ai_validation}).  Across all individual criteria, the
lenient acceptance rate (Pass~+~Minor issue) exceeds 95\,\%.  When
all five criteria are considered jointly, 187 of 200 extracted
sections satisfy the acceptance standard, corresponding to an overall
success rate of 93.5\,\% (95\,\%~CI: 89.2--96.2\,\%).

Failures are concentrated in older filings: five of the six occur in
2010--2012 and one in 2022--2024, consistent with SEC filing formats
becoming more standardised over time. The extraction is accurate,
and the remaining errors are mostly in early filings with less
standard document structure.

\paragraph{Relation to prior approaches.}
Our extraction strategy is motivated by \textcite{dyer2017}, who
document large changes in 10-K disclosures over time and the
problems these cause for large-sample textual analysis. Compared
with the plain-text extraction used in earlier studies
\parencite[e.g.][]{campbell2014,li2008,loughran2011}, the HTML-based
parser produces fewer false positives from TOC entries, navigation
elements, and inline cross-references. Unlike approaches based on
XBRL-tagged text blocks (e.g.\ \texttt{us-gaap:RiskFactorsTextBlock}),
it also covers a much longer period: these tags became more reliable
after the adoption of Inline XBRL but are incomplete and inconsistent
in earlier filings. Extracting sections from the HTML lets us apply
the same method over the full 2010--2024 sample.
