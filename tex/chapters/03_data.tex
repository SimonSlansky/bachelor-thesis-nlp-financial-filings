% =============================================================================
\chapter{Data}
\label{ch:data}
% =============================================================================

This chapter describes how the firm-year panel is built. Financial variables
come from the SEC EDGAR Company Facts API, the narrative sections are parsed
from the HTML of the filings on EDGAR, and return-based measures are computed
from daily prices downloaded from Yahoo Finance with the \texttt{yfinance}
package. No commercial research database (such as Compustat, CRSP or a vendor
10-K corpus) is used. Building the data from these sources means that the
textual and financial data can be matched to the same filing, period and
reporting entity, and that the processing steps are visible in the code that
accompanies the thesis. It also means that problems that commercial providers
usually handle (XBRL tag heterogeneity, irregular HTML, amendments, share
counts of multi-class firms) are dealt with directly; this chapter describes
those choices and their consequences. Yahoo Finance prices are not raw: the
adjusted close used for returns is adjusted for splits and dividends by the
provider, and the price series were downloaded once and kept fixed for all
computations. The definition, source and timing of every variable are
listed in the accompanying data dictionary (Appendix~\ref{app:variables}).

Figure~\ref{fig:data_pipeline} gives an overview. The pipeline starts from
the SEC sources, applies the sample filters, and runs a financial (XBRL)
branch and a textual branch that are merged into the firm-year panel.

\section{Sample selection}
\label{sec:sample_selection}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The starting universe is the first 1{,}000 entries in the SEC file
\path{company_tickers.json}, which maps stock tickers to Central Index Keys
(CIKs). The version used in this study was archived in the project repository
in October 2026. Four filters are then applied (Table~\ref{tab:attrition} in
Appendix~\ref{app:variables}). First, only entities classified by the SEC as
operating companies are retained, which excludes exchange-traded funds,
closed-end funds, and shell companies (1{,}000 to 753). Second, firms with SIC
codes 6000--6999 (finance, insurance, and real estate) are excluded. Following
\textcite{fama1992}, financial firms are omitted because leverage and related
balance-sheet ratios have a different economic interpretation for such firms
than for non-financial operating companies, as financial assets and
liabilities constitute a core component of their business model rather than
primarily a financing choice. This filter reduces the sample from 753 to 600
firms. Third, firms without a fiscal-year end date in the metadata are
dropped (600 to 599). Fourth, firms must have at least five fiscal years of
available 10-K XBRL data. This requirement ensures that lagged variables, such
as asset growth, can be constructed while still leaving at least four years of
usable panel observations. The filter reduces the sample to 550 firms using
current metadata; the frozen universe used in the final analysis contains 548
firms. These firms form the panel of 6{,}757 firm-years for fiscal years
2010--2024, and the 30-day estimation sample is the 5{,}782 firm-years of 512
firms that remain after the requirements below (Table~\ref{tab:attrition}).
Figure~\ref{fig:data_pipeline} shows the same stages.

For the pre-filing liquidity screen, a filing requires at least 200 daily closing prices in the 252 trading days before filing. Separately, its outcome window must contain enough returns to calculate volatility. The first rule can be applied on the filing date. The second determines whether the outcome can be measured.

\paragraph{Scope of the sample.} The October 2026 list favours firms still listed and large at that date. Companies acquired or delisted earlier may be absent, and the number of observations varies by year. The results describe this unbalanced sample of large surviving firms.

\section{Annual filings as the unit of observation}
\label{sec:annual_vs_quarterly}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The unit of observation is one annual filing by one firm. An annual 10-K provides the full risk-factor discussion. A quarterly 10-Q can refer readers back to the annual discussion when risks have not materially changed. In the 15-firm pilot, only 52\% of quarterly filings contained a substantive risk-factor section (Appendix~\ref{sec:freq_diagnostics}). Treating the remaining filings as short disclosures would confuse the quarterly reporting format with a firm's choice about how much risk to disclose. Annual reports also provide full-year accounting figures alongside the text.

\section{Financial data from XBRL}
\label{sec:xbrl_tags}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

\paragraph{Point-in-time values.} For each firm and fiscal period the panel
takes the values, the filing date and the accession number of the
\emph{first} 10-K that reports facts for that period end, filed within 180
days of it. Restated figures published later (in a 10-K/A or as comparatives
in the next year's 10-K) are not mixed in, because they were not public when
the post-filing window starts. A 10-K/A is used only when no original 10-K
reports the period. All variables of a firm-year therefore come from one
filing, identified by its accession number, which also identifies the
filing whose text is used. The fiscal year is the calendar year in which the
period ends (periods ending on 1--7 January count to the previous year, for
firms with 52/53-week years).

\paragraph{Fiscal years and calendar time.} Firms do not share a common
fiscal calendar, so 10-K filings from the same fiscal year are released at
different points in calendar time (Table~\ref{tab:timing} in
Appendix~\ref{app:variables}). In the estimation sample, 72.6\% of
firm-years end in December, while 145 of the 512 firms have fiscal year-ends
between January and November. The median lag between fiscal-period end and
filing is 53 days (10th to 90th percentile: 38 to 59 days), which places most
filings in February. This concentration reflects the sample's focus on large
firms, which typically file annual reports relatively soon after fiscal
year-end. Despite this clustering, filing dates for a given fiscal year are
dispersed over more than a full calendar year. To ensure comparability across
firms, all timing variables are anchored to each firm's own filing date.
Post-filing volatility windows, pre-filing volatility windows, and the
lagged-volatility control are all defined relative to the firm's filing day
rather than a common calendar date (Section~\ref{sec:outcome}). Accounting
variables and textual measures are likewise taken from the filing that opens
the measurement window. Fiscal years are assigned using the actual
reporting-period end rather than the fiscal-year-end month currently reported
by the SEC, which proved unreliable for some 52/53-week fiscal-year firms and
had shifted the fiscal-year labels of approximately 20 firms in an earlier
version of the panel. In addition, lagged observations are matched only when
the previous filing corresponds to the same fiscal year one year earlier,
preventing changes in fiscal-year ends from creating lags of unequal length.
Finally, because fiscal-year fixed effects cannot fully absorb calendar-time
shocks when filings from the same fiscal year are spread across many months,
the robustness checks replace fiscal-year fixed effects with filing-month fixed
effects and restrict the sample to December year-end firms
(Section~\ref{sec:robustness_h12}).

\paragraph{Tag choices.} One accounting concept can be reported under several
US-GAAP elements, so the elements must be mapped explicitly
\parencite{chychyla2015}. Two kinds of alternatives are handled differently.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Equivalent tags} measure the same quantity and are used
    interchangeably. Net income is measured on a consolidated basis:
    \texttt{ProfitLoss} (including non-controlling interests, NCI) where a
    firm reports it, and \texttt{NetIncomeLoss} otherwise, as a proxy where the consolidated tag is unavailable. This makes ROA consistent
    with total assets, which also include NCI.
  \item \emph{Approximately equivalent tags.} The two operating-cash-flow
    tags (total and continuing operations) differ when a firm has cash flows
    from discontinued operations, so they are not strictly equivalent. The
    panel uses the total-operations tag where it exists and the
    continuing-operations tag otherwise, without locking, and accepts the
    difference as measurement error in one control variable. The size of
    this error is reported below.
  \item \emph{Non-equivalent tags}, such as stockholders' equity with or
    without NCI, or income from continuing operations versus net income, are
    \emph{locked} per firm: each firm keeps the tag it reports in most years,
    and values under other tags are set to missing. The choice uses the
    firm's whole history, a mild look-ahead in which tag is used, not in the
    values.
\end{itemize}

Table~\ref{tab:tag_provenance} in Appendix~\ref{app:variables} reports the
tag choices, the values lost to locking (637 of 7{,}900 equity values, 38
net-income values, no cash-flow values) and the differences between paired
tags in firm-years that report both. Where both net-income tags are present
(3{,}838 firm-years), the median absolute difference is 0.043\% of total
assets, but the 99th percentile is 3.56\% and the maximum 37.4\%; in 303
firm-years (7.9\%) the difference exceeds 1\% of total assets, which we call
material. These discrepancies show why the fallback can introduce measurement error in ROA. For the two
operating-cash-flow tags (417 firm-years) the difference exceeds 1\% of
assets in 50 firm-years (12.0\%), with a median of 0.035\%. The cash-flow
differences are therefore material in a non-trivial share of the overlapping
firm-years, which is why the two tags are not called equivalent above. Operating cash flow over assets is one of nine controls.

\paragraph{Missing balance-sheet values.} If total liabilities are missing,
they are computed as total assets minus stockholders' equity (2{,}171
firm-years; 1{,}935 in the 30-day regression sample). The accounting identity requires equity including non-controlling interests (NCI). If only parent equity is available, assets minus equity includes any NCI as well as liabilities. In the regression sample, the parent-only calculation is used for 60 firms. The data do not show how many of these firms have non-zero NCI.
Where they do, leverage is overstated by the NCI share of total assets. The
check without imputed values in Section~\ref{sec:robustness_h12} removes all
of these firm-years. If total assets or liabilities are still missing, the
value of the immediately preceding fiscal year is carried forward (1 and 32
firm-years); values are never filled from later years. Each imputation is
flagged, and Section~\ref{sec:robustness_h12} re-estimates the models without
imputed values.

\paragraph{Book equity.} Book-to-market uses book equity attributable to the
parent, which matches the market
capitalisation of the parent's common shares. When this tag is missing, total
equity including NCI is used (430 of 5{,}782 firm-years in the 30-day
sample), and when that is missing too, total assets minus total liabilities
(55 firm-years). The remaining firm-years use parent equity.

\paragraph{Market capitalisation.} Shares outstanding are taken from the same
10-K as the other variables, matched by accession number: the cover-page
count (summed over share classes), else the balance-sheet count, else the fiscal-year weighted-average
basic count. The last source is needed for multi-class firms whose
cover-page counts are reported per class and are not available in the
Company Facts API (445 firm-years); a robustness check drops these
firm-years. Shares are multiplied by the as-traded closing price on the last
trading day before the filing, which is rebuilt from the split-adjusted
price and the split history.

\section{Text extraction from 10-K filings}
\label{sec:text_extraction}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The pipeline extracts Item~1A (Risk Factors) and Item~7 (Management's Discussion and Analysis, MD\&A). The three main hypotheses concern Item~1A. Item~7 is included in the extraction audit and in a supplementary length control.

\subsection{Source of the text}
\label{ssec:text_source}

For each original 10-K in the panel the primary HTML document was downloaded
from the EDGAR archive, using the document name listed in the EDGAR
Submissions API.\footnote{%
\texttt{https://data.sec.gov/submissions/CIK\{cik\}.json}; see
\url{https://www.sec.gov/search-filings/edgar-application-programming-interfaces}.}
Where the filing incorporates a section by reference to an exhibit, the
exhibit was fetched from the same filing (Section~\ref{ssec:section_id}).
All requests carried the declared user-agent header that the SEC requires
and stayed below its limit of ten requests per second. The extraction was
run once, in October 2026; the stored texts were not re-extracted for this
version, and each stored section is linked to the accession number of the
filing it came from. In seven firm-years the stored text comes from a 10-K/A
whereas the panel now uses the original 10-K; these firm-years are excluded
from all text regressions.

\subsection{Section identification and exhibit fallback}
\label{ssec:section_id}

The text of a 10-K is embedded in HTML without standard tagging, mixed with
table-of-contents entries, running headers and cross-references, so a plain
search for ``Item~1A'' typically finds the table of contents. The parser
works on the document structure before the text is flattened:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item \textbf{Candidate detection.} Elements (paragraphs, divisions, table
    cells, headings) whose text matches an ``Item~X'' pattern are kept as
    candidate headers if they have at most 25 words, carry a heading cue
    (bold font, all capitals, or a font size of at least 12\,pt), and contain
    no hyperlink (table-of-contents links).
  \item \textbf{In-place tagging.} Each candidate is marked inside the HTML
    tree, and only then is the document converted to text, so header
    positions survive the conversion.
  \item \textbf{Candidate selection.} A candidate for Item~1A must have the
    word ``risk'' within 300 characters, and one for Item~7 ``management'',
    ``discussion'' or ``MD\&A''. Candidates with at least three other item
    headers within 300 characters (a table of contents) and candidates
    preceded by ``see'', ``refer to'' or similar phrases are discarded when
    alternatives exist. Among the rest the parser takes the candidate
    followed by the longest section.
  \item \textbf{Section bounding.} The section ends at the next item header:
    Item~1B, 1C or 2 for Item~1A, and Item~7A, 8, 9, 9A or 9B for Item~7.
    Several terminators are needed because some items are optional (Item~1B
    applies only to some filers, and Item~1C exists only for fiscal years
    ending on or after 15~December~2023). A section needs at least 200 words.
\end{enumerate}

Figure~\ref{fig:dom_parser} illustrates the four steps on a simplified DOM
tree.

\input{figures/fig_dom_parser}

The parser is intentionally conservative: it does not operate on a flattened
HTML string, but on the DOM tree, where heading structure and placement can be
tracked before the text is reduced to plain running text. The same logic is
also used for the exhibit fallback described below. The implementation
details of the candidate-detection and fallback logic are reported in
Appendix~\ref{sec:parser_impl}.

\paragraph{Exhibit fallback.} If no section of at least 200 words is found
and the text under the header (fewer than 500 words) refers to an exhibit or
the annual report, the parser applies the same procedure to the referenced
exhibit (usually Exhibit~13), using title patterns (``Risk Factors'',
``Management's Discussion and Analysis'') because exhibits have no item
numbers. It does not read the page references given in the filing. The
extraction run did not record which sections came from an exhibit, so the
share cannot be measured without downloading the filings again.

\subsection{Post-processing and tokenisation}
\label{ssec:text_postprocessing}

During the HTML conversion, scripts, style sheets and the hidden inline-XBRL
header are removed, and tables are removed if they are numerical: at least
30\% of their non-empty cells (with at least four cells and two rows) are
bare numbers or currency amounts, or more than 40\% of their characters are
digits or number symbols. Other tables, which in many filings are used for
page layout, keep their text. After the section has been bounded, the parser
removes running ``Table of Contents'' headers, page footers such as ``2019
Form 10-K 59'', standalone page numbers, residual numeric table rows (lines
of at least four tokens of which more than 40\% are numbers), and short lines
that repeat at least four times (running headers), and replaces non-breaking
spaces. Footnote markers and text of layout tables are not removed.

For the dictionary measures the text is lowercased and split into tokens of
at least two letters (digits, hyphens and apostrophes split tokens); a
section with fewer than 100 tokens is treated as missing. The category
definitions and financial-text motivation follow \textcite{loughran2011}; the
word-level input is the Loughran--McDonald Master Dictionary, 1993--2025
release \parencite{loughran_mcdonald_2025}. I count words with a positive
year-added category code and exclude words marked with a negative removal
code. The resulting lists contain 297 uncertainty and 903 litigious words;
the two lists share no word.

\subsection{Coverage}
\label{ssec:text_coverage}

Among the 6{,}757 firm-years in the panel, 96.3\% have an Item~1A section
with at least 100 tokens, and 94.9\% have an Item~7 section of the same
minimum length (Table~\ref{tab:text_coverage} in
Appendix~\ref{app:variables}). Coverage is lowest in fiscal year 2010
(92.8\%), and it rises to 94.7\% in 2024. The lower 2024 figure reflects a
correction to the fiscal-year labels of about 15 firms with 52/53-week
calendars; their fiscal-year-2024 filings had not yet been extracted.

\paragraph{Extraction audits.} Two large language models (LLMs) independently
audited a stratified random sample of stored sections. The sample contains
five three-year strata (2010--2012 to 2022--2024), with 20 sections of each
type per stratum, for 200 sections in total. Item~1A and Item~7 were drawn
independently, so the 200 sections come from 197 filings. Each section was
rated on five criteria: correct section, start boundary, end boundary,
content quality and completeness. Ratings were recorded as Pass, Minor issue
or Fail. The first audit, by Google Gemini~2.5~Flash, saw the full
extracted section; the second, by Claude Opus~5.5, saw the first and last
1{,}200 characters, a middle excerpt and the word count, without the first audit's ratings. Neither model saw the rest of the filing, so the boundary
judgments were based only on the extracted text. I also checked 51 sections by hand
against the original EDGAR filings, without seeing the LLM ratings: 50 were
drawn at random within the same strata, and one additional section was
flagged by an LLM.

Under the first audit, 187 of 200 sections (93.5\%, Wilson 95\% interval
89.2--96.2\%) were acceptable on all five criteria (Pass or Minor), and 179
(89.5\%) were rated Pass on all five. The 13 sections with a Fail include
five with no extracted text. By stratum, five fall in 2010--2012, three in
2013--2015, two each in 2016--2018 and 2019--2021, and one in 2022--2024.
Under the second audit, 190 sections (95.0\%, 91.0--97.3\%) were acceptable.
Both audits reject the same eight sections (Table~\ref{tab:ai_validation} in
Appendix~\ref{app:ai-usage}). In the manual check, all 51 sections were the
correct section. Four had a boundary error: two started inside Item~1, one
ran a few lines into the financial statements, and one ran through Item~8.
Three of those errors were among the 50 random sections, implying an error
rate of 6.0\% (Wilson 95\% interval 2.1--16.2\%). The manual verdict on the
three criteria matches the first audit on all 51 sections and the second on
50. Errors are more common in early filings, whose structure is less
standardised.

The exhibit fallback addresses filings that incorporate part of the annual report by reference \parencite[General Instruction G(2)]{sec_form10k_2014}. Its contribution to overall coverage was not recorded. Overall, the parser uses document structure to reduce section-identification
errors while retaining the risk that headings with atypical formatting may be
missed.
