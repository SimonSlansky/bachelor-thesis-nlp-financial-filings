\section{Descriptive statistics}
\label{sec:descriptive}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Table~\ref{tab:summary} summarises the 30-day estimation sample of 5{,}782
firm-years. The median annualised 30-day post-filing volatility is 0.243
(mean 0.317). The median Item~1A has 8{,}700 tokens, but a few sections are
much longer (the 99th percentile is 35{,}579). The mean RiskDensity is 0.0511,
which means that about one token in twenty is an uncertainty or litigious
word. Uncertainty words (mean density 0.0346) are about twice as common as
litigious words (0.0165).

\input{tables/summary_statistics}

Figure~\ref{fig:vol_time} shows the median 30-day post-filing volatility by
fiscal year, together with the inter-quartile range. Volatility is highest
for fiscal year 2019. Those 10-Ks were filed in early 2020, just before and
during the market turmoil of spring 2020. The fiscal-year fixed effects
remove such common shocks as long as they affect all filings of the same
fiscal year.

\caption{Median annualised 30-day post-filing volatility by fiscal year,

Table~\ref{tab:correlation} shows the pairwise correlations. Length and
RiskDensity are negatively correlated ($r = -0.30$): longer sections contain
a smaller share of dictionary risk words, mostly uncertainty words
($r = -0.44$). The two sub-densities are almost uncorrelated ($r = -0.07$).
Pre-filing and post-filing volatility are correlated at 0.53, which is why
pre-filing volatility is the most important control.

\section{Length and risk-word density (H1 and H2)}
\label{sec:joint_results}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Table~\ref{tab:main_30d} shows five nested specifications estimated on the
same sample: controls only (1), length (2), density (3), length and density
together (4), and the split into the two sub-lists (5).

\input{tables/main_30d}

\paragraph{H1.} In the joint specification (column 4), a longer Item~1A goes
together with higher post-filing volatility: $\hat\beta^{\text{len}} =
+0.053$ ($t = +4.51$). The coefficient is similar in columns 2 and 5, and it
stays significant under a wild cluster bootstrap by year ($p = 0.001$;
Table~\ref{tab:inference}). \textbf{H1 is supported} in the industry
fixed-effects design. However, Section~\ref{sec:robustness_firmfe} shows that
the corresponding firm fixed-effects coefficient is not statistically
significant.

\paragraph{H2.} When length is held fixed, the composite RiskDensity has a
negative coefficient ($\hat\beta^{\text{dens}} = -1.845$, $t = -1.70$,
$p = 0.090$; wild bootstrap $p = 0.092$). Without the length control
(column 3) it is $-3.408$ ($t = -3.33$). Length and density are negatively
correlated, so part of the effect in column 3 is really a length effect.
H2 is a two-sided test, and the composite coefficient is not significantly
different from zero at the 5\% level. So \textbf{H2 is not supported at the
30-day horizon}. At longer horizons the picture changes: from 90 days on, the
composite coefficient is negative and significant at the 5\% level
($t = -3.33$ at 365 days; Table~\ref{tab:main_365d}).

\paragraph{Sub-lists.} In column 5, uncertainty density is not related to
volatility ($\hat\beta^{\text{unc}} = -0.545$, $t = -0.31$), while litigious
density has a negative coefficient ($\hat\beta^{\text{lit}} = -3.018$,
$t = -2.90$; wild bootstrap $p = 0.005$). The Wald test cannot reject that
the two coefficients are equal ($p = 0.179$), so we cannot say that the two
sub-lists really behave differently. Still, the negative sign of the
composite comes mainly from the litigious words. This fits the boilerplate
channel of Section~\ref{sec:h2} better than the risk-content channel, but
the design cannot show which feature of litigious language is responsible.

\paragraph{Magnitudes.} Table~\ref{tab:effects} puts the coefficients on a
common scale. A one pooled standard deviation increase in
$\ln(\text{Words}^{1A})$ (0.65 log points) goes together with 3.8\% higher
30-day volatility. A one pooled standard deviation increase in LitDensity
goes together with 1.4\% lower volatility, and for the composite RiskDensity
the figure is $-1.2\%$. Changes within a single firm are much smaller (one
within-firm standard deviation of length is 0.27 log points), so the same
coefficients imply 1.6\% and $-0.6\%$ for within-firm changes. At the median
volatility of 0.243, a 3.8\% change is about 0.9 percentage points of
annualised volatility. The text variables explain very little additional
variance: the within-$R^2$ only rises from 0.363 with controls alone to 0.368
(Table~\ref{tab:main_30d}).

\input{tables/effect_sizes}

\paragraph{365-day horizon.} Table~\ref{tab:main_365d} repeats the five
columns with the 365-day window on the same firm-years. Length is still
positive ($+0.084$, $t = +8.64$ in column 5), LitDensity is still negative
($-4.709$, $t = -5.38$), and UncDensity is not significant. At this horizon
the Wald test does reject that the two sub-list coefficients are equal
($p < 0.001$).

\section{The omission gap (H3)}
\label{sec:silence_results}
% ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Table~\ref{tab:silence_main} shows the H3 specification at six horizons and
with both fixed-effect structures, for the omission gap and for its
length-invariant version.

\input{tables/silence_main}

At the 30-day horizon the omission coefficient is $+0.081$ ($t = +0.37$)
with industry fixed effects and $+0.475$ ($t = +0.70$) with firm fixed
effects. None of the ten coefficients in the family of tests is significant,
even before the Holm adjustment, and all Holm-adjusted $p$-values equal one.
The length-invariant gap tells the same story ($+0.487$, $t = +1.60$, and
$+0.351$, $t = +0.58$). Following the decision rule of Section~\ref{sec:h3},
\textbf{H3 is not supported}: once length, density and the controls are held
fixed, the omission gap is not related to post-filing volatility.
Chapter~\ref{ch:robustness} shows that no variant of the measure changes this
conclusion.
