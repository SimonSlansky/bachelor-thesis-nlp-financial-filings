\section{Descriptive Statistics}
\label{sec:descriptive}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Table~\ref{tab:summary} reports summary statistics for the regression
sample.  The dependent variable, post-filing annualised volatility, has a
mean of about 0.33 and a standard deviation of 0.17, showing large
cross-sectional differences in firm-level risk.  The interquartile range
(0.22 to 0.39) points to a right-skewed distribution, consistent with
occasional spikes during market stress periods such as 2020.

Lagged volatility has a nearly identical distribution, as expected for a
persistent series.  Firm size, measured as $\ln(\text{Total Assets})$,
ranges from mid-sized (P25~$\approx$~22.1) to large companies
(P75~$\approx$~24.2).  Leverage centres around 0.61 with moderate spread.
ROA has a mean near 0.06, and asset growth averages about 0.12 with a
long right tail driven by acquisitive firms.

\input{tables/summary_statistics}

Table~\ref{tab:correlation} presents pairwise Pearson correlations.
Volatility and its lag are strongly positively correlated ($r = 0.61$),
confirming persistence.  Firm size correlates negatively with volatility
($r \approx -0.32$), in line with the well-known size--risk relationship.
ROA also correlates negatively with volatility ($r \approx -0.33$),
while leverage and asset growth show weaker univariate links.
None of the pairwise correlations among the explanatory variables
exceed 0.40 in absolute value, reducing multicollinearity concerns
(variance inflation factors are below 1.4 for all regressors).

\section{Baseline: Financial Determinants of Volatility}
\label{sec:baseline}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Table~\ref{tab:baseline} reports OLS estimates of post-filing
annualised volatility on progressively richer sets of financial
controls, estimated as described in Section~\ref{sec:estimation}.
The three columns correspond to nested specifications: lagged
volatility only (Column~1), with firm size and leverage added
(Column~2), and the full baseline including return on assets and
asset growth (Column~3).

\input{tables/baseline_regression}

\paragraph{Volatility persistence.}
Column~(1) includes only lagged volatility ($\sigma_{i,t}$) alongside the
fixed effects.  The coefficient of $0.67$ ($t = 33.5$) confirms the
well-documented persistence of firm-level return volatility: a one
percentage-point rise in last year's annualised volatility maps into
about a two-thirds percentage-point rise the following year.  This single
regressor, combined with industry and year fixed effects, already
explains about $63\%$ of the cross-sectional and time-series variation
in post-filing volatility, showing that most of the predictable component
of risk is mechanical.

\paragraph{Firm size and capital structure.}
Column~(2) adds firm size, measured as the natural logarithm of total
assets, and book leverage (total liabilities over total assets).  Size
enters with a negative and highly significant coefficient
($-0.017$, $t = -7.4$): larger firms exhibit lower future volatility,
consistent with the size--risk relationship documented for U.S.\ equities
by \textcite{chi2017} and \textcite{campbell2014}.  Leverage is
economically negligible and statistically indistinguishable from zero
($t = 0.17$); the industry fixed effects appear to absorb most of the
cross-sectional dispersion in capital structure, leaving little residual
variation through which leverage can operate.

\paragraph{Profitability and growth.}
The full specification in Column~(3) adds return on assets and asset
growth.  ROA carries a large negative coefficient ($-0.224$,
$t = -7.9$), confirming that profitable firms face lower subsequent
volatility, in line with the earnings-quality intuition of
\textcite{francis2005}.  Asset growth enters positively
($+0.026$, $t = 3.1$): rapid expansion is associated with elevated
future uncertainty, consistent with the operational-risk channel.
Adding these two regressors raises the adjusted~$R^2$ from $0.643$ to
$0.654$---a modest but statistically meaningful improvement that refines,
rather than dominates, the persistence-and-size benchmark.

A noteworthy by-product of the full specification is that the lagged
volatility coefficient declines from $0.67$ in Column~(1) to $0.55$
($t = 20.7$) in Column~(3).  Roughly one-fifth of what looked like raw
persistence is in fact mediated by slower-moving fundamentals---size,
profitability, and growth---that are themselves persistent.  Lagged
volatility nonetheless remains by a wide margin the most informative
single regressor in the model.

\paragraph{Benchmark for textual analysis.}
The full baseline model explains $65.4\%$ of the variation in
post-filing volatility.  This adjusted~$R^2$ is the bar that the
text model and the divergence model must clear, on the criteria
listed in Section~\ref{sec:hypothesis_tests}.  The bar is high:
financial fundamentals together with volatility persistence already
capture most of the predictable variation in firm-level risk,
leaving narrative disclosures to compete for what remains.

\section{Textual Features and Volatility (H1)}
\label{sec:text_results}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Table~\ref{tab:text_regression} adds the three textual features to
the full baseline.  Column~(1) reproduces the financial baseline.
Column~(2) adds the two within-firm dictionary measures,
$\Delta$Sentiment and $\Delta$Risk.  Column~(3) further adds TextSim,
the cosine similarity of the firm's TF-IDF vector with its own
prior-year filing.

\input{tables/text_regression}

\paragraph{Sentiment dominates.}
Of the three textual variables, only $\Delta$Sentiment carries
information about future volatility.  Its coefficient is positive and
strongly significant ($+2.04$, $t = 4.23$ in Column~3): firms whose
MD\&A tone became more positive than usual go on to experience
higher post-filing volatility.  Read against the prior of
\textcite{loughran2011}, who find that more pessimistic 10-K tone
predicts higher returns and volatility, the positive sign here may
look surprising.  It is reconciled in the next section, where the
divergence variable separates the part of $\Delta$Sentiment that
tracks contemporaneous earnings from the part that does not.

\paragraph{Risk-word change adds nothing.}
$\Delta$Risk is essentially zero ($-0.012$, $t = -0.02$).  This is
consistent with evidence that the Item~1A risk section has become
heavily templated since the 2005 SEC mandate
\parencite{campbell2014}: year-on-year changes in risk-word density
within a firm carry little new information once industry and year
fixed effects are absorbed.

\paragraph{TextSim is borderline.}
The textual-similarity measure carries a small positive coefficient
($+0.063$, $t = 1.86$, significant at the 10\% level only).
Mechanically, a value close to one means that this year's filing
reads almost identically to last year's, which one might expect to
\emph{lower} subsequent uncertainty.  The weakly positive sign goes
in the opposite direction and is not robust to the inclusion of the
divergence variable, so we do not over-interpret it.

\paragraph{Joint significance.}
A Wald test of $H_0\!: \boldsymbol{\gamma} = \mathbf{0}$ on the
three textual coefficients in Column~(3) rejects strongly
($\chi^2(3) = 20.51$, $p = 0.00013$), and the adjusted~$R^2$ rises
from $0.643$ to $0.645$.  The improvement in fit is small in
absolute terms because the financial baseline already explains the
bulk of predictable volatility, but the joint test confirms that
textual disclosures contribute information that is statistically
distinct from financial fundamentals.  Both criteria for H1 listed
in Section~\ref{sec:hypothesis_tests} are therefore met:
\textbf{H1 is supported}.

\section{Textual--Financial Divergence (H2)}
\label{sec:divergence_results}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The central test of the thesis is reported in
Table~\ref{tab:divergence_regression}.  Column~(1) again repeats the
financial baseline; Column~(2) is the full text model from the
previous section; Column~(3) adds the Divergence variable defined
in equation~\ref{eq:divergence}.

\input{tables/divergence_regression}

\paragraph{The divergence coefficient.}
Divergence enters with a negative coefficient of $-4.85$
($t = -1.86$, $p = 0.063$) on the one-year horizon.  The sign goes
in the direction predicted by H2 under the credible-signalling
reading set out in Section~\ref{sec:hypothesis_tests}: tone that
moves more positively than concurrent earnings warrant lowers
post-filing volatility, consistent with investors treating the
gap as a costly and therefore credible signal of management's
private information.  At the one-year horizon the result is only
marginally significant; the next subsection shows that significance
strengthens sharply once shorter post-filing windows are used, a
pattern revisited in detail in Chapter~\ref{ch:robustness}.

\paragraph{Why $\Delta$Sentiment changes when divergence is added.}
The coefficient on $\Delta$Sentiment more than triples between
Column~(2) and Column~(3) (from $+2.04$ to $+6.57$).  This is not
instability but mechanics: by construction, Divergence is the part
of $\Delta$Sentiment that is orthogonal to $\Delta$ROA within firm
(Section~\ref{ssec:divergence}).  Once the orthogonal part is
controlled for explicitly, the residual $\Delta$Sentiment
coefficient measures the volatility response to the part of tone
that \emph{does} co-move with concurrent earnings.  The two effects
go in opposite directions and partially cancelled in Column~(2).
Splitting them clarifies the pattern: tone that tracks fundamentals
raises volatility (consistent with operating-risk amplification),
while tone that decouples from fundamentals lowers it (consistent
with credible signalling).

\paragraph{Incremental fit.}
Adding Divergence raises the adjusted~$R^2$ from $0.6443$ to
$0.6448$.  The improvement is small in absolute terms, as is to be
expected when the dependent variable is annual volatility on top of
a baseline that already captures persistence and fundamentals; but
it is in the predicted direction, and the much larger improvements
at shorter horizons (Section~\ref{sec:robustness_horizons}) confirm
that divergence carries economic information.  H2 is therefore
supported on the second criterion of
Section~\ref{sec:hypothesis_tests} (incremental $R^2$) and on the
sign criterion; the strict 5\%-significance criterion is met at
every horizon shorter than one year (see
Table~\ref{tab:horizons}), which we read as overall support for H2
across the relevant economic time scales.

\section{Economic Magnitude}
\label{sec:magnitude}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The Divergence variable has a within-firm standard deviation of
about $0.0026$ on the regression sample.  Combining this with the
estimated coefficients gives the implied effect of a
one-standard-deviation rise in Divergence on post-filing
annualised volatility:
\begin{itemize}
  \item at the 5-day horizon: $-18.13 \times 0.0026 \approx -0.047$,
        i.e.\ a drop of about $4.7$ percentage points of annualised
        volatility, or roughly $19\%$ of the sample mean
        ($\bar{\sigma}_{5d} = 0.255$);
  \item at the 30-day horizon: $-13.91 \times 0.0026 \approx
        -0.036$, about $11\%$ of the sample mean of vol\textsubscript{30d};
  \item at the one-year horizon: $-4.85 \times 0.0026 \approx
        -0.013$, about $4\%$ of the sample mean of annual volatility.
\end{itemize}
The effect is therefore economically meaningful in the days
immediately after the filing and decays smoothly with horizon, in
line with the timing intuition that any credible-signalling channel
should operate first and decay as new information arrives.  The
five-day effect of roughly five percentage points of annualised
volatility is comparable in size to the cross-sectional effect of
moving a firm from the median to the 25th percentile of size, and
so is well within the range of effects routinely reported in the
disclosure literature \parencite{loughran2011, campbell2014}.

The same picture is visible without any model: sorting the
estimation sample into quartiles by Divergence and computing the
average post-filing 30-day volatility within each quartile yields a
near-monotone decline from the lowest to the highest quartile, with
the top quartile averaging roughly two percentage points of
annualised volatility lower than the bottom quartile.  The model
estimate and the model-free sort agree on direction and on rough
magnitude, which provides reassurance that the regression result is
not driven by a small subset of influential observations.
