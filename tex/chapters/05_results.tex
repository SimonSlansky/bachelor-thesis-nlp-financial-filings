\chapter{Results}
\label{ch:results}
% =============================================================================

This chapter reports the empirical results. Section~\ref{sec:descriptive}
summarises the regression sample. Section~\ref{sec:joint_results}
presents the headline test of H1 and H2 at the 30-day horizon and
introduces the H3 boilerplate-decomposition column.
Section~\ref{sec:annual_results} reproduces the same specification
at the 365-day horizon to confirm that the results are not an
artefact of the short-window choice.
Section~\ref{sec:horizons_results} runs the H1+H3 specification at
every horizon between 5 and 365~days, and
Section~\ref{sec:boilerplate_results} discusses the H3 evidence
formally. Section~\ref{sec:magnitude} translates the estimated
coefficients into economic magnitudes.

\section{Descriptive statistics}
\label{sec:descriptive}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Table~\ref{tab:summary} reports summary statistics for the regression
sample at the 30-day horizon. The dependent variable is post-filing
volatility in logs (equation~\ref{eq:vol}); its distribution is
approximately symmetric with mean
$\ln \sigma_{i,t+1}^{[30]} \approx -1.34$ and standard deviation
$\approx 0.59$, and an interquartile range running from $-1.74$ to
$-1.06$. In raw units this corresponds to a typical annualised
post-filing volatility around $0.26$ and a P25--P75 band of roughly
$0.18$--$0.35$, in line with the right-skewed distribution typical
of equity volatility.

Lagged log-volatility has a near-identical distribution as expected
for a persistent series. Firm size is measured as the natural log of
total assets in dollars, with a sample mean of $23.18$ (roughly
$\$11.7$~billion in assets) and an interquartile range from
$22.19$ to $24.24$ (roughly $\$4.4$ to $\$33.6$~billion); this
confirms the focus on large, liquid firms motivated in
Section~\ref{sec:sample_selection}. Leverage centres around $0.62$
with moderate spread; ROA averages $0.06$ with a long left tail; and
asset growth averages $0.11$ with a long right tail driven by
acquisitive firms.

The two text variables show economically meaningful spread.
$\ln(\text{Words}^{1A})$ has a mean of $9.03$ and a standard
deviation of $0.64$, corresponding to a median Item~1A of roughly
$8{,}800$~words and an interquartile range from $\approx 5{,}650$
to $\approx 12{,}300$~words. RiskDensity has a mean of $0.051$ and
a standard deviation of $0.006$: roughly $5\%$ of the words in a
typical Item~1A come from the LM uncertainty or litigious lists.
The two LM sub-components used for H3 are unevenly sized: the
uncertainty share averages $0.034$ ($\approx 68\%$ of
RiskDensity) and the litigious share averages $0.016$ ($\approx
32\%$). The two sub-shares are essentially uncorrelated
($r \approx -0.05$ in the regression sample), confirming that the
decomposition delivers two genuinely separate regressors.

\input{tables/summary_statistics}

Table~\ref{tab:correlation} reports pairwise Pearson correlations.
Lagged log-volatility is the strongest single predictor of
log-volatility ($r = 0.35$). Larger firms have lower log-volatility
($r \approx -0.19$) and higher profitability also tracks lower
log-volatility ($r \approx -0.23$), in line with the well-known
size--risk and earnings-quality relationships. The two text
variables are correlated at $r = -0.30$: longer Item~1A sections
tend to have lower risk-word density, consistent with their
narrative being padded by firm-specific text rather than by extra
generic risk vocabulary. The negative correlation is moderate and
leaves both signals separately identifiable in the joint
specification.

\section{Volume, specificity, and boilerplate at the 30-day horizon}
\label{sec:joint_results}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Table~\ref{tab:main_30d} reports the headline test of H1, H2, and
H3. Column~(1) is the financial baseline; Column~(2) adds Item~1A
length in logs (the volume channel); Column~(3) adds the composite
risk-word density (the specificity channel); Column~(4) is the joint
specification of equation~\ref{eq:joint_model} that nests H1 and H2;
Column~(5) is the H3 decomposition specification of
equation~\ref{eq:decomposition_model}, which replaces RiskDensity by
its two LM sub-components.

\input{tables/main_30d}

\paragraph{The baseline (Column~1).} Lagged log-volatility carries
the expected positive coefficient ($+0.170$, $t = +8.65$): a
one-percent rise in last-year's volatility maps into about
$0.17\%$ extra volatility this year. Firm size enters negatively
($-0.070$, $t = -10.32$): larger firms are less volatile.
Profitability is strongly stabilising ($\text{ROA}$
coefficient~$-1.07$, $t = -12.82$); asset growth raises future
volatility ($+0.246$, $t = +9.03$); leverage is statistically
indistinguishable from zero, as in much of the volatility
literature \parencite{campbell2014}. The baseline already explains
$49\%$ of the in-sample variation in log-volatility.

\paragraph{The volume channel (Columns~2, 4, and~5).} Adding
$\ln(\text{Words}^{1A})$ alone (Column~2) raises adjusted~$R^2$ to
$0.505$ and yields a length coefficient of $+0.138$ ($t = +8.79$).
In the joint specification (Column~4), where the composite
risk-word density is controlled for, the length coefficient is
$+0.121$ ($t = +7.35$). In the H3-decomposition specification
(Column~5), where the composite is replaced by its two LM
sub-components, the length coefficient rises slightly to $+0.130$
($t = +7.70$). The result is unambiguous: longer risk-factor
sections predict higher post-filing log-volatility at the 30-day
horizon. The point estimate of $0.130$ from Column~(5)
corresponds to roughly an $8\%$ increase in post-filing
volatility for a one-standard-deviation rise in
$\ln(\text{Words}^{1A})$ ($0.64 \times 0.130 = 0.083$, applied to
$\ln \sigma$). \textbf{H1 is supported.}

\paragraph{The specificity channel (Columns~3 and~4).} Adding
RiskDensity alone (Column~3) yields a coefficient of $-7.78$
($t = -6.13$). In the joint specification, where the volume channel
is controlled for, the coefficient remains negative and significant
at $-3.88$ ($t = -2.74$, $p = 0.006$). The fact that the density
coefficient halves once length is controlled for is exactly the
mechanical pattern anticipated in
Section~\ref{ssec:why_both}: about half of what looked like a
``risk-density effect'' in Column~(3) was in fact the volume effect
operating through the section's word-count denominator. Once
$\ln(\text{Words}^{1A})$ is partialled out, the residual density
effect is purely specificity in the \textcite{hope2016} sense: a
denser, more boilerplate Item~1A is associated with \emph{lower}
post-filing volatility. \textbf{H2 is supported}, and the joint
specification reveals an effect that the dictionary-only ratio used
in much prior work would have hidden.

\paragraph{The boilerplate decomposition (Column~5).} Replacing
the composite RiskDensity by its two LM sub-components reveals
that the negative specificity effect of Column~(4) is carried
entirely by the LM-Litigious sub-list. LitDensity enters with a
coefficient of $-6.16$ ($t = -3.35$, $p < 0.001$), while
UncDensity enters with a small and statistically insignificant
coefficient of $-1.28$ ($t = -0.68$, $p = 0.50$). A formal
$t$-test of $H_0: \beta^{\text{unc}} = \beta^{\text{lit}}$ rejects
at the 5\%~level. Adjusted $R^2$ in Column~(5) is essentially
identical to that of the composite specification in Column~(4)
($0.5068$ vs.\ $0.5064$), because the litigious sub-list captures
the variance that drives the composite. The H2 specificity effect
is therefore not an effect of generic ``risk vocabulary'' as the
composite would suggest, but specifically of cautionary,
legal-process language of the kind that
\textcite{nelson2007,cazier2021} associate with mature, legally
supervised disclosure environments. \textbf{H3 is supported.}

\paragraph{Joint significance and incremental fit.} The joint test
of $H_0: \beta^{\text{len}} = \beta^{\text{unc}} = \beta^{\text{lit}}
= 0$ in Column~(5) rejects with $F(3, \cdot) > 40$. Adding the
three text variables to the baseline raises adjusted $R^2$ from
$0.492$ (Column~1) to $0.507$ (Column~5), an increment that is
small in absolute terms because the financial baseline already
captures the bulk of predictable volatility but is large by the
standards of the textual-disclosure literature
\parencite{kravet2013, campbell2014}. The three text variables are
informative both individually and jointly.

\section{The same channels at the 365-day horizon}
\label{sec:annual_results}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Table~\ref{tab:main_365d} re-estimates the same five nested
specifications with the dependent variable replaced by
$\ln \sigma_{i,t+1}^{[365]}$, the log of annualised volatility over
the full year following the filing. The lagged dependent variable
is the analogous one-year window for the prior filing year.

\input{tables/main_365d}

The signs and significance pattern of all three text variables
survive at the annual horizon. In the H1+H2 joint specification
(Column~4) $\beta^{\text{len}} = +0.056$ ($t = +7.01$) and
$\beta^{\text{dens}} = -2.19$ ($t = -3.50$, $p < 0.001$). In the
H1+H3 decomposition (Column~5) $\beta^{\text{len}} = +0.061$
($t = +7.35$), $\beta^{\text{lit}} = -3.39$ ($t = -4.38$,
$p < 0.001$), and $\beta^{\text{unc}} = -0.86$ ($t = -0.99$,
$p = 0.32$). What changes between the 30-day and 365-day windows is
the magnitude: the length coefficient is roughly half of the
30-day estimate; the litigious coefficient is roughly $55\%$ of the
30-day estimate. Both shrink toward zero as the volatility window
lengthens, exactly as the event-study evidence of
\textcite{loughran2011} would predict, but neither sign nor
significance reverses. The split between an active LitDensity and
a null UncDensity is preserved.

\section{Multi-horizon table}
\label{sec:horizons_results}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Table~\ref{tab:horizons} reports the H1+H3 decomposition
specification of equation~\ref{eq:decomposition_model} at six
post-filing horizons: $h \in \{5, 10, 30, 90, 180, 365\}$ calendar
days. Each column is a separate regression on the full panel;
financial controls and fixed effects are unchanged.

\input{tables/horizons}

Three patterns emerge. First, $\ln(\text{Words}^{1A})$ enters with
a positive and highly significant coefficient at every horizon,
ranging from $+0.195$ at 5~days to $+0.061$ at 365~days, with
$t$-statistics between $+7.06$ and $+8.36$. The volume channel is
robust across horizons. Second, LitDensity enters with a negative
coefficient at every horizon and is significant at the 5\% level
from 5~days onward and at the 1\% level from 10~days onward; the
$t$-statistics range from $-2.28$ at 5~days to $-4.44$ at 90~days.
Third, UncDensity is small in absolute value and statistically
indistinguishable from zero at every horizon, with $|t| \leq 1$ at
all windows except 5~days. The qualitative pattern of
Column~(5) of Table~\ref{tab:main_30d} therefore replicates at
every horizon the data admit: the negative effect of risk-word
language is a litigious-language effect, not an uncertainty-language
effect.

The lagged-dependent-variable coefficient also rises with horizon,
from about $0.030$ at 5~days to $0.566$ at 365~days, reflecting the
mechanical fact that longer windows for both the dependent and
the lagged variable share more of their underlying daily-return
sample. This is a feature of the data, not of the specification.

\section{The boilerplate decomposition}
\label{sec:boilerplate_results}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The headline result of Column~(5) of Table~\ref{tab:main_30d} and
the consistent across-horizon pattern of
Table~\ref{tab:horizons} together support a sharp interpretation
of what the H2 ``specificity effect'' actually is. The composite
RiskDensity used in much of the prior literature
\parencite{loughran2011, kravet2013} pools two LM word lists with
very different production technologies. Our decomposition shows
that the negative association of RiskDensity with post-filing
volatility is driven entirely by the LM-Litigious sub-list. Once
the litigious component is partialled out, the LM-Uncertainty
component has no incremental association with volatility at any
horizon.

The magnitude is economically meaningful. A one-standard-deviation
rise in LitDensity ($\approx 0.005$) corresponds to a
$0.005 \times 6.16 \approx 2.8\%$ decrease in 30-day annualised
volatility. The interquartile range of LitDensity
($\approx 0.006$) implies an $\approx 3.8\%$ swing in volatility
from the lowest to the highest litigious-density quartile.

The interpretation aligns with the boilerplate-disclosure literature
reviewed in Section~\ref{sec:lit_boilerplate}.
\textcite{nelson2007} and \textcite{cazier2021} document that
litigious-list language in mandatory filings is dominated by
standardised cautionary boilerplate inserted by legal counsel
under the safe-harbour regime of the 1995 Private Securities
Litigation Reform Act, while \textcite{loughran2015} stress that
uncertainty and litigious wordings are conceptually distinct
constructs. Firms whose Item~1A is heavier in litigious wording
are therefore plausibly firms with more institutionalised
compliance functions and more legally supervised disclosure
processes, characteristics that prior work
\parencite{nelson2007, cazier2021, beatty2019} associates with
lower realised risk. The Item~1A length effect (H1) and the
litigious-density effect (H3) are then two distinct
firm-level signals: the former captures how much management has
chosen to disclose, the latter captures the legal-procedural style
in which it is disclosed.

A caveat on identification is reserved for
Section~\ref{sec:robustness_firmfe} of the next chapter.
\textbf{H3 is supported in the cross-section}, with the LitDensity
sign holding at every horizon and on both COVID sub-samples;
whether it survives within-firm absorption is examined separately
in the robustness chapter.

\section{Economic magnitude}
\label{sec:magnitude}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Table~\ref{tab:main_30d} translates straightforwardly into
economic magnitudes because the dependent variable is in logs and
the regressors are either in logs or are bounded densities.

\paragraph{Length.} A one-standard-deviation rise in
$\ln(\text{Words}^{1A})$ ($\approx 0.64$, corresponding to a
$\approx 90\%$ increase in word count) implies an
$0.64 \times 0.130 \approx 8.3\%$ increase in 30-day annualised
volatility (using the Column~5 estimate). Going from the 25th to
the 75th percentile of Item~1A length implies an
$0.78 \times 0.130 \approx 10.1\%$ increase in volatility. Relative
to a sample-mean annualised volatility of $\approx 0.26$, this is
roughly $2.6$~percentage points of annualised volatility---an
economically substantive effect well within the range of
disclosure effects reported in
\textcite{loughran2011, campbell2014}.

\paragraph{Composite specificity (H2).} A one-standard-deviation
rise in RiskDensity ($\approx 0.006$) implies a
$0.006 \times (-3.88) \approx -2.3\%$ change in 30-day annualised
volatility. The interquartile range of RiskDensity is
$\approx 0.008$, implying an $\approx -3.1\%$ swing in volatility
from the lowest to the highest density quartile.

\paragraph{Litigious sub-component (H3).} A one-standard-deviation
rise in LitDensity ($\approx 0.005$) implies a
$0.005 \times (-6.16) \approx -2.8\%$ change in 30-day annualised
volatility---of the same order of magnitude as the composite H2
effect, despite LitDensity being only one-third of RiskDensity by
share. The fact that the entire composite effect can be relocated
onto a sub-list one-third its size is the empirical content of the
H3 decomposition: the negative ``specificity'' effect of generic
risk vocabulary is in fact concentrated in cautionary
legal-process boilerplate.
\paragraph{Why the dictionary-only ratio understates the
specificity channel.} The univariate Column~(3) coefficient on
RiskDensity is $-7.78$, twice the Column~(4) joint estimate of
$-3.88$. The reason is the negative correlation between the two
text variables (Section~\ref{sec:descriptive}): longer Item~1A
sections tend to be slightly less dense, so omitting length from
the regression loads part of the (positive) volume effect onto a
variable ($\text{RiskDensity}$) that is itself negatively
correlated with length, exaggerating the negative coefficient.
The joint specification of equation~\ref{eq:joint_model}
disentangles the two channels and the H3 decomposition
(equation~\ref{eq:decomposition_model}) localises the resulting
effect inside the LM lexicon.
