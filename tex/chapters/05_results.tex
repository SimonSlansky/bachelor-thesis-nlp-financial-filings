\section{Descriptive statistics}
\label{sec:descriptive}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Table~\ref{tab:summary} summarises the 30-day estimation sample of 5{,}782
firm-years. The median annualised 30-day post-filing volatility is 0.243
(mean 0.317). The median Item~1A has 8{,}700 tokens, but a few sections are
much longer (the 99th percentile is 35{,}579). The mean RiskDensity is 0.0511,
which means that about one token in twenty is an uncertainty or litigious
word. Uncertainty words (mean density 0.0346) are about twice as common as
litigious words (0.0165).

Figure~\ref{fig:vol_time} shows the median 30-day post-filing volatility by
fiscal year, together with the inter-quartile range. Volatility is highest
for fiscal year 2019. Many of those 10-Ks were filed in early 2020. Some post-filing windows therefore included the market turmoil of spring 2020. Fiscal-year effects account for the average difference across fiscal years, while firms filing in different months can still face different market conditions.

RiskDensity are negatively correlated ($r = -0.30$): longer sections contain

Log pre-filing and log post-filing volatility have a correlation of 0.53,

\subsection*{Delta Air Lines during COVID-19}
Delta's annual report filed on 13 February 2020 shows how the accounts and the risk section can describe different moments (Table~\ref{tab:delta_case}). In fiscal 2019, operating cash flow was 13.1\% of assets. By the filing date, Delta had suspended flights to China. Item~1A described the suspension and warned that a wider outbreak could reduce demand for air travel \parencite{delta_fy2019}. Investors already knew about the suspension from Delta's announcement on 31 January \parencite{delta_china}. Further news followed during the volatility window. On 10 March, Delta announced capacity cuts and measures to preserve cash \parencite{delta_march}. From the close on 18 February to the close on 19 March, Delta shares lost 63.1\%. The annualised volatility of their 22 daily returns was 144.7\%, compared with 79.3\% for the market benchmark. These figures reflect a month that included both company news and large market movements.

\input{tables/delta_case}

A year later, Delta described how travel restrictions and falling demand had affected its entire network \parencite{delta_fy2020}. Operating cash flow had turned negative. Item~1A grew from 5{,}851 to 8{,}133 words, yet volatility after the filing fell to 34.8\%. The number of dictionary risk words also rose, from 252 to 322. Their share fell from 4.31\% to 3.96\% because the rest of the section grew faster. In this case, reading the warning identifies the business risk that a word count alone cannot show. \textcite{davis2020covid} likewise use the content of earlier risk sections to study how firms' stock returns differed during the pandemic.

\section{Length and risk-word density (H1 and H2)}
\label{sec:joint_results}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Table~\ref{tab:main_30d} shows five specifications estimated on the
same sample: controls only (1), length (2), density (3), length and density
together (4), and the split into the two sub-lists (5).

\input{tables/main_30d}

\paragraph{H1.} In the joint specification (column 4), a longer Item~1A goes
together with higher post-filing volatility: $\hat\beta^{\text{len}} =
+0.053$ ($t = +4.51$). The coefficient is similar in columns 2 and 5, and it
stays significant under a wild cluster bootstrap by year ($p = 0.001$;
Table~\ref{tab:inference}). \textbf{H1 is supported} in the industry
fixed-effects design. Does the same firm also have more volatile returns in years when it writes a longer risk section? In that comparison the joint-model length estimate falls to $+0.008$ and is imprecise (Table~\ref{tab:inference}). Firms with longer sections tend to have higher volatility, but the data give little evidence of a similar year-to-year association within a firm.

\paragraph{H2.} When length is held fixed, the composite RiskDensity has a
negative coefficient ($\hat\beta^{\text{dens}} = -1.845$, $t = -1.70$,
$p = 0.090$; wild bootstrap $p = 0.092$). Without the length control
(column 3) it is $-3.408$ ($t = -3.33$). Length and density are negatively
correlated, so the density-only coefficient also reflects differences in disclosure length.
H2 is a two-sided test, and the composite coefficient is not significantly
different from zero at the 5\% level. So \textbf{H2 is not supported at the
30-day horizon}. At longer horizons the picture changes: from 90 days on, the
composite coefficient is negative and significant at the 5\% level
($t = -3.33$ at 365 days; Table~\ref{tab:main_365d}).

\paragraph{Sub-lists.} In column 5, the estimate for uncertainty density gives little evidence of an association with
volatility ($\hat\beta^{\text{unc}} = -0.545$, $t = -0.31$), while litigious
density has a negative coefficient ($\hat\beta^{\text{lit}} = -3.018$,
$t = -2.90$; wild bootstrap $p = 0.005$). A direct test gives $p=0.179$ for the difference between the two coefficients. At 30 days the evidence is too weak to conclude that legal words and uncertainty words have different associations with volatility. A negative litigious coefficient alone
cannot establish that legal vocabulary measures boilerplate. The dictionary 
counts the same word in a routine warning and in a description of a specific lawsuit.

\paragraph{Magnitudes.} Table~\ref{tab:effects} puts the coefficients on a
common scale. A one pooled standard deviation increase in
$\ln(\text{Words}^{1A})$ (0.65 log points) goes together with 3.8\% higher
30-day volatility. A one pooled standard deviation increase in LitDensity
goes together with 1.4\% lower volatility, and for the composite RiskDensity
the figure is $-1.2\%$. These magnitudes use the split-list specification 
for length and litigious density and the composite specification for RiskDensity. 
They describe differences in fitted volatility. At the median
volatility of 0.243, a 3.8\% change is about 0.9 percentage points of
annualised volatility. The text variables explain very little additional
variance: the within-$R^2$ only rises from 0.363 with controls alone to 0.368
(Table~\ref{tab:main_30d}).

columns with the 365-day window on the same firm-years. Length is still

\section{The omission gap (H3)}
\label{sec:silence_results}
% ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

At 30 days, the full-text omission coefficient is $+0.081$ with industry fixed effects ($t=0.37$) and $+0.475$ with firm fixed effects ($t=0.70$). Using blocks of text gives $t=1.60$ and $t=0.58$, respectively. None of the four estimates reaches the 5\% threshold, even before adjustment for multiple tests. \textbf{H3 is not supported.} Table~\ref{tab:silence_main} in the appendix reports all horizons.
