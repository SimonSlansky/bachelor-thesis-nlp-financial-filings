\section{Alternative horizons}
\label{sec:robustness_horizons}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Table~\ref{tab:horizons} re-estimates the composite and decomposition
specifications with volatility over 5, 10, 30, 90, 180 and 365 days. The
length coefficient is positive and significant at the 1\% level at every
horizon (between $+0.058$ and $+0.101$ in the decomposition). LitDensity is
negative and significant from 30 days on ($t$ between $-2.90$ and $-5.38$),
but not at 5 or 10 days, where the windows contain few returns. UncDensity
is never significant. The composite RiskDensity is significant at the 5\%
level from 90 days on. Figure~\ref{fig:horizon} plots the length and
LitDensity coefficients. The coefficients are not compared formally across
horizons, because the windows overlap and the estimates are correlated.

\input{tables/robustness_30d}

The length result is highly stable: the coefficient remains positive and
statistically significant across the alternative market, time, industry,
sample, timing, and inference specifications reported in Table~\ref{tab:robust_30d}.
The LitDensity result is more sensitive. Its significance weakens when
firm-years with imputed balance-sheet observations are removed, in the
December-only sample, and when counts replace density ratios; the remaining
checks leave the substantive conclusion unchanged. Thus H1 is robust across
the tested specifications, whereas the evidence for H2 depends more on the
measurement and sample definition.

\section{Firm fixed effects}
\label{sec:robustness_firmfe}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The headline models compare firms within an industry and year. Replacing the
industry effects by firm effects asks a different question: whether a firm
whose Item~1A becomes longer or denser than usual has higher volatility
than usual. Table~\ref{tab:firm_fe} reports the answer, and
Figure~\ref{fig:coef_fe} compares the coefficients.

\input{tables/firm_fe}

Under firm fixed effects the length coefficient falls from $+0.058$ to
$+0.009$ ($t = +0.45$), and no density coefficient is significant
(LitDensity $-3.132$, $t = -1.02$). Adding the lagged dependent variable,
whose coefficient is biased under firm fixed effects \parencite{nickell1981},
changes nothing (column 3). The associations in Chapter~\ref{ch:results} are
therefore between-firm patterns: firms with longer and more litigious
risk-factor sections differ systematically in subsequent volatility from
otherwise similar firms in the same industry and year, but year-to-year
changes in a firm's own section do not track changes in its volatility. Two
features of the data limit what the firm fixed-effects models can show.
First, most of the variation in the text variables is between firms: only
18\% of the variance of $\ln(\text{Words}^{1A})$ and 19\% of that of
LitDensity is within firms (Table~\ref{tab:variance}), so the within
estimates are imprecise; the confidence interval of the within-firm
LitDensity coefficient contains the between-firm estimate. Second, Item~1A
changes slowly from year to year in this sample: only 18\% of the variance
of log Item~1A length and 19\% of LitDensity is within firms (Table~5.5).
This is a finding from the panel, not a direct implication of the MD\&A study
of \textcite{brown2011} or the legal and borrowing evidence in
\textcite{cazier2021}.

\section{The omission measure (H3)}
\label{sec:robustness_silence}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

H3 is not supported in Chapter~\ref{ch:results}. This section reports three
diagnostics that clarify the construction and interpretation of the measure.
Table~\ref{tab:silence_diag} summarises them.
\input{tables/silence_diag}

\paragraph{Length.} The omission gap is strongly related to length:
$r = -0.915$ with $\ln(\text{Words}^{1A})$ in the H3 sample of 4{,}659
firm-years (Figure~\ref{fig:omission_length}), indicating that the measure
is closely tied to disclosure length.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{img/fig_omission_length.pdf}
\caption{Omission gap against $\ln(\text{Words}^{1A})$, one point per
firm-year of the 30-day H3 sample, with the OLS fit.}
\label{fig:omission_length}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Sources of variation.} The decomposition in
Table~\ref{tab:silence_diag} attributes 91\% of the year-on-year change to
the firm's own text, 6\% to peer-text changes, and 3\% to peer composition.

\paragraph{Placebo.} Firms were reassigned at random to industry cells within
each fiscal year, keeping the cell sizes, the gap was recomputed and the
firm fixed-effects model re-estimated, 1{,}000 times. The observed
$t$-statistic of $+0.70$ lies in the middle of the placebo distribution: the
empirical two-sided $p$-value is 0.466, and 2.4\% of the
placebo $t$-statistics exceed 1.96 (Figure~\ref{fig:placebo}). The real peer
groups carry no more information about volatility than random ones.

\section{Persistence and unit roots}
\label{sec:robustness_stationarity}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

With at most 15 annual observations per firm, the dynamic panel raises two
separate concerns: finite-$T$ bias in specifications with a lagged dependent
variable, and possible non-stationarity. The Nickell bias discussed in
Section~\ref{sec:estimation} does not establish that the second concern is
negligible. Table~\ref{tab:stationarity} therefore reports two panel unit-root
tests designed for short panels. Every variable is first demeaned by fiscal
year; this removes a common additive year component but is not a general cure
for cross-sectional dependence. The Harris--Tzavalis test
\parencite{harris1999} uses the balanced panel of firms observed in all years
2012--2024 and tests the null that every firm's series has a unit root against
a common autoregressive coefficient below one. The Im--Pesaran--Shin test
\parencite{ips2003} averages firm-level augmented Dickey--Fuller statistics
on the unbalanced panel; its null is that all firms have a unit root, and a
rejection means that at least some firms' series are stationary, not that all
are. Both procedures rely on their panel-specific innovation, dependence and
ADF-specification assumptions. The simulated $p$-values reported here are
adaptations calibrated to the observed sample dimensions, rather than values
supplied by the cited papers.

\input{tables/stationarity}

Both tests reject the unit-root null for volatility, the text variables and
most controls. Two variables are exceptions. For leverage the
Harris--Tzavalis test does not reject ($\hat\rho = 0.772$, $p = 0.592$),
while the Im--Pesaran--Shin test does; for the omission gap neither test
rejects at the 5\% level ($\hat\rho = 0.758$, $p = 0.257$; IPS $p = 0.099$). Leverage is
a control, and the omission gap enters only the H3 models, whose coefficient
is insignificant. A ratio bounded between zero and one cannot follow a random
walk forever, but over 13 years it can be persistent enough that these tests
lack power; the results are therefore read as showing high persistence for
these two variables rather than as proof of stationarity.
