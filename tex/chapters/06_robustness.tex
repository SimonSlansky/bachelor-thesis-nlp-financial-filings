\section{Alternative post-filing windows}
\label{sec:robustness_horizons}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The headline specification measures post-filing volatility over a
30-day window. To check the choice of window we re-estimate the
H1+H2 specification of equation~\ref{eq:decomposition_model} at
five additional horizons (5, 10, 90, 180, and 365~days).
Table~\ref{tab:horizons} reports all six columns side by side.

\input{tables/horizons}

All three text coefficients keep the predicted sign at every
horizon: positive for $\ln(\text{Words}^{1A})$, negative for
LitDensity, and not significantly different from zero for
UncDensity. Neither H1 nor the LM-list decomposition reverses for
any of the windows. The length coefficient is significant at the
1\%~level at every horizon. The LitDensity coefficient is
significant at the 1\%~level from 10~days onward, with $|t|$
between $2.76$ (10~days) and $4.19$ (180~days). At 5~days it has
the same sign but is not significant ($-4.15$, $t = -1.36$). A
5-day window holds only a few daily returns, so the volatility
estimate is noisy and the sample is smaller (5{,}234~firm-years).
The UncDensity coefficient is never significant ($|t| \leq 1.21$
at every window). The 30-day window is not one in which the
results happen to be strongest.

Both coefficient series decline in a similar way with $h$,
consistent with a signal that is priced over weeks rather than
years. We do not fit a formal half-life because the error terms
across horizons are correlated through the shared daily returns.

Figure~\ref{fig:horizon} plots the two headline coefficients with
their 95\% confidence intervals across all six horizons. The
length effect (left) is positive and significant throughout, and
the litigious-density effect (right) is negative throughout and
significant from 10~days onward. Both become smaller at longer
windows.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{img/fig_horizon_profile.pdf}
\caption{Length (H1, left) and LM-Litigious density (H2, right)

\section{Pre-COVID and COVID-era sub-samples}
\label{sec:robustness_subperiods}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The 2020 fiscal year and its aftermath were a large shock to both
equity volatility and corporate disclosure. Average annualised
volatility roughly doubled in spring 2020, and Item~1A sections
grew longer as firms added pandemic and supply-chain language. We
re-estimate the H1+H2 decomposition specification
(equation~\ref{eq:decomposition_model}) separately for the two
periods. Filings with fiscal year $\leq 2019$ form the pre-COVID
sample (3{,}595~firm-years) and filings with fiscal year $\geq 2020$
form the COVID-era sample (2{,}453~firm-years). All other features
of the specification are unchanged. Table~\ref{tab:subperiod}
reports the result.

\input{tables/subperiods}

Length and LitDensity have the predicted signs and similar
magnitudes in both periods. Length is
significant at the 1\% level in both ($\beta = +0.100$, $t = +5.99$
pre-COVID; $\beta = +0.168$, $t = +8.58$ COVID era). LitDensity is
negative and significant in both ($\beta = -5.86$, $t = -2.74$
pre-COVID; $\beta = -10.18$, $t = -5.64$ COVID era). UncDensity is
insignificant in both, as in the full sample. Length and
LitDensity coefficients are larger in absolute value in the
COVID-era sample, consistent with the higher level of volatility
in that period, but the pattern is the same. The main results are
not driven by COVID.

\section{Within-firm identification: firm fixed effects}
\label{sec:robustness_firmfe}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The headline specification absorbs two-digit SIC industry and
fiscal-year fixed effects, so coefficients come from variation
\emph{within an industry-year cell}. This removes industry
differences in risk vocabulary and macroeconomic time effects, but
persistent firm-level traits could still drive the result. We
replace the industry fixed effects with firm fixed effects, so
that identification comes only from \emph{within-firm} year-to-year
variation in the three text variables. Table~\ref{tab:firm_fe}
reports the result at the 30-day horizon. Standard errors are
clustered by both firm and fiscal year.

\input{tables/firm_fe}

The length effect remains, though it is smaller:
$\hat{\beta}^{\text{len}} = +0.069$ ($t = +2.57$, $p \approx 0.01$).
Firms that lengthen their Item~1A from one year to the next have
higher subsequent volatility. Both LM sub-densities become
insignificant: $\hat{\beta}^{\text{unc}} = -2.39$ ($t = -0.92$) and
$\hat{\beta}^{\text{lit}} = -1.04$ ($t = -0.26$). The mix of risk
vocabulary changes little within firms because Item~1A is largely
carried over from one filing to the next \parencite{cazier2021},
so there is little variation left to estimate a sub-density
coefficient from. This fits the \textcite{cazier2021,beatty2019}
view of cautionary boilerplate as a near-permanent firm-level
disclosure style. We therefore interpret the H2 result as a
between-firm pattern and do not claim a within-firm causal
channel.

Figure~\ref{fig:coef_fe} shows the contrast. Under industry and
year fixed effects the length coefficient is positive, both density
coefficients are precisely estimated, and LitDensity is below zero.
With firm and year fixed effects the length effect is almost
unchanged, but the density intervals widen and both include zero.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.85\textwidth]{img/fig_coef_fe.pdf}
\caption{H1 and H2 coefficients at the 30-day horizon with 95\%
confidence intervals, under industry$+$year fixed effects (circles)
versus firm$+$year fixed effects (squares). The length effect is
stable across the two designs; the density coefficients lose
significance once firm fixed effects absorb permanent disclosure
styles.}
\label{fig:coef_fe}
\end{figure}

\section{Robustness of the strategic-silence result (H3)}
\label{sec:robustness_silence}
% ────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The H3 result of Section~\ref{sec:silence_results} comes from a
within-firm specification at the 30-day horizon. Three features of
the design call for further checks before reading it as evidence of
strategic silence:
(i)~the omission gap is mechanically negatively correlated with
Item~1A length ($r = -0.93$), so with a weak length control the
omission gap could proxy for length;
(ii)~the SIC2 peer benchmark is one of several possible reference
sets, and the result might depend on that choice;
(iii)~the filing date and the start of the volatility window are
close together, which leaves room for reverse causality if a
difficult year produces both a shorter, less peer-aligned 10-K and
higher volatility for unrelated reasons.
Table~\ref{tab:silence_robust} reports six specifications that
address these concerns; a placebo test is reported separately
below.

Figure~\ref{fig:omission_length} illustrates the first concern.
Longer Item~1A sections cover more of the peer vocabulary, so the
omission gap falls steeply with length ($r = -0.93$). Column~(3)
of Table~\ref{tab:silence_robust}, which removes the length
component from the omission gap by orthogonalising it on
$\ln(\textsc{Words}_{1A})$, is therefore the key check for H3.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{img/fig_omission_length.pdf}
\caption{Item~1A omission gap against Item~1A length, one point per
firm-year, with the OLS fit line. The strong negative association
($r = -0.93$) is the central identification concern for H3 and
motivates the length-orthogonalised specification in Column~(3) of
Table~\ref{tab:silence_robust}. Firm-years with non-recoverable
Item~1A text ($\textsc{Words}_{1A} = 0$, $n = 63$) are excluded from
the figure: these observations mechanically take
$\textsc{Omission}_{1A} = 1$ and do not enter any regression because
the LM-density variables are undefined for them.}
\label{fig:omission_length}
\end{figure}

\input{tables/silence_robust}

\paragraph{Placebo (peer-cell shuffle).} The first check keeps the
SIC2 cell sizes and the firm-year distribution fixed but randomly
reassigns firms to SIC2 cells within each fiscal year, recomputes
the omission gap of equation~\ref{eq:omission}, and re-estimates
equation~\ref{eq:silence_model} with firm and year fixed effects.
If the association reflects silence on \emph{peer-relevant} risks,
the placebo coefficient should disappear. If it is a length
artefact, the placebo should resemble the real coefficient. Across
five permutations the placebo $t$-values are
$1.00, 0.59, 2.19, 1.20, 1.39$. Four of the five are small, but one
($t = 2.19$) is significant at the 5\%~level. The real coefficient
($t = +2.58$) is only a little above the largest placebo value. So
the placebo test gives only weak support: a random peer group can
sometimes produce a similar association, most likely because the
omission gap still carries some length information.

\paragraph{Length controls (Columns 3 and 5).}
Column~(3) replaces the omission gap by its residual after
regressing on $\ln(\textsc{Words}_{1A})$ within each (SIC2,
fiscal year) cell; the residual has the predicted sign but is not
significant ($t = +1.54$, $p = 0.12$). Column~(5) keeps the original
gap but adds two more length controls (MD\&A word count and squared
Item~1A log word count); the omission coefficient stays significant
($t = +2.85$, $p = 0.004$). Linear or quadratic length controls do
not remove the effect, but once the length part of the gap is
fully removed (Column~3) the effect is no longer significant.

\paragraph{Large-cell restriction (Column 4) and prior-year
specification (Column 6).} Restricting to cells with at least
20~peers reduces the panel to $3{,}396$~firm-years and
\emph{increases} the coefficient to $+2.47$ ($t = +3.01$), so the
effect is stronger where the peer aggregate is measured more
precisely. Column~(6) uses prior-year omission to address the
filing-window overlap; the lagged coefficient is positive but
significant only at the 10\%~level ($+0.448$, $t = +1.88$,
$p = 0.06$).

\paragraph{Decile rank (Column 2).} Using the decile rank of
omission within each (SIC2, fiscal year) cell keeps the positive
sign but is not significant ($t = +0.74$). If there is an effect,
it sits in the tails of the omission distribution, where
omissions are most visible.

\paragraph{Summary for H3.} The omission coefficient has the
predicted positive sign in every column. It stays significant with
extra length controls and in the large-cell sample. However, it is
not significant once the length part is fully removed (Column~3)
or with the decile rank (Column~2), it is only significant at the
10\%~level with prior-year omission (Column~6), and one of five
placebo runs comes close to the real $t$-value. We therefore treat
the H3 result as suggestive only: it is consistent with a
within-firm strategic-silence channel, but we cannot rule out
that it is partly a length effect, and we make no causal claim.

\section{Stationarity of the panel}
\label{sec:robustness_stationarity}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Every specification in this thesis is a dynamic panel: lagged
log-volatility enters equation~\ref{eq:joint_model} and its
extensions with its own coefficient $\phi$. If the outcome or a
regressor had a unit root, the regression could be spurious. We
test all twelve regression variables for stationarity with the
(augmented) Dickey--Fuller test.

\paragraph{How the test is applied.}
Each variable is a panel of short annual series, with at most
fifteen observations per firm. We adapt the
\textcite{dickeyfuller1979} test in two steps. First, we subtract
each year's cross-sectional mean from every variable, which removes
common shocks such as the 2020 volatility spike, as the year fixed
effects do in the regressions. Second, we run an augmented
Dickey--Fuller regression (intercept, one lag) for each firm and
combine the per-firm $p$-values with Fisher's method
\parencite{maddala1999}. Pooling is needed because a single
fifteen-year series has little power.
Table~\ref{tab:stationarity} reports, for each variable, the
average autoregressive coefficient, the average ADF statistic, the
share of firms that reject the unit root individually, and Fisher's
combined $p$-value.

\input{tables/stationarity}

\paragraph{Autoregressive coefficients.}
A unit root means $\rho = 1$. The estimates of $\hat\rho$ range
from $-0.07$ for the (demeaned) outcome to at most $0.64$, far from
one. After removing the common market factor, firm-specific
log-volatility is almost serially uncorrelated ($\hat\rho \approx
0$), and the most persistent variables (leverage, Item~1A length,
and the risk-word densities) have $\hat\rho$ below two-thirds.

\paragraph{Per-firm and pooled tests.}
The average ADF statistic lies between $-1.6$ and $-2.6$, short of
the $-2.9$ needed to reject at $5\%$ for a single series, and only
$10$--$30\%$ of firms reject individually. This is expected given
fifteen-year series. The pooled Fisher test rejects the unit-root
null with a $p$-value below $0.001$ for all twelve variables. The
Dickey--Fuller test without the augmentation lag rejects more often
(the share of firms rejecting roughly doubles), so the result does
not depend on the lag choice.

\paragraph{Bounded variables.}
The variables with the highest $\hat\rho$, leverage and the
risk-word densities, are bounded by construction: leverage lies
between zero and one, and the densities are word shares with sample
maxima below $0.05$. A unit-root process has variance that grows
without bound, so boundedness is evidence against a unit root,
though not a proof of stationarity. Together with the test results
above, it makes an integrated process implausible. The persistence
of these variables reflects the fact that Item~1A is largely
carried over from one filing to the next \parencite{cazier2021}, a
slow-moving but mean-reverting firm trait, as in the firm
fixed-effects result of Section~\ref{sec:robustness_firmfe}.

\paragraph{Summary.} All regression variables have autoregressive
coefficients well below one, and the pooled Dickey--Fuller test
rejects the unit-root null for all twelve at the $0.1\%$ level. The
panel is stationary, $I(0)$, and the main associations are not
spurious regressions.
