\section{Alternative horizons}
\label{sec:robustness_horizons}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Table~\ref{tab:horizons} re-estimates the composite and decomposition
specifications with volatility over 5, 10, 30, 90, 180 and 365 days. The
length coefficient is positive and significant at the 1\% level at every
horizon (between $+0.058$ and $+0.101$ in the decomposition). LitDensity is
negative and significant from 30 days on ($t$ between $-2.90$ and $-5.38$),
but not at 5 or 10 days, where the windows contain few returns. UncDensity
is never significant. The composite RiskDensity is significant at the 5\%
level from 90 days on. Figure~\ref{fig:horizon} plots the length and
LitDensity coefficients. The coefficients are not compared formally across
horizons, because the windows overlap and the estimates are correlated.

\section{Robustness of the H1 and H2 estimates}
\label{sec:robustness_h12}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Table~\ref{tab:robust_30d} reports the decomposition specification under
alternative choices, one change per row (Table~\ref{tab:robust_365d} in
Appendix~\ref{app:variables} repeats it at 365 days).

\input{tables/robustness_30d}

\paragraph{Market conditions and earnings releases.} Adding the market's
volatility over the same window, replacing the fiscal-year fixed effects by
filing-month $\times$ fiscal-year effects, and using idiosyncratic volatility
as the dependent variable leave the length coefficient between $+0.053$ and
$+0.089$ and LitDensity between $-2.235$ and $-3.399$, all significant at the
5\% level. Earnings releases rarely fall in the 30-day window, because the
release usually precedes the 10-K; dropping the windows that contain one,
or removing the release day and the following day from every window, does
not change the estimates.

\paragraph{Fiscal and calendar time.} The filings of one fiscal year span a
median of 390 calendar days (Table~\ref{tab:timing}), so the fiscal-year
effects do not absorb calendar-time shocks. Replacing them by effects for
the calendar month of the filing (169 months) leaves the length coefficient
at $+0.057$ ($t = +4.94$) and LitDensity at $-2.329$ ($t = -2.62$). In the
sample of the 371 firms with a December fiscal-year end, where fiscal and
calendar time coincide (4{,}200 firm-years), length is $+0.060$
($t = +4.64$) and LitDensity $-1.906$ ($t = -1.96$), significant only at the
10\% level: the length result holds in both, while the litigious-density
result weakens when the sample is restricted.

\paragraph{Industry definition.} Finer (SIC3) or alternative
(Fama--French 48) industry effects, today's rather than the historical SIC
code, and excluding utilities (SIC~49) and health services (SIC~80), whose
risk disclosures are shaped by regulation, all leave both coefficients
significant; with Fama--French industries LitDensity is $-4.052$
($t = -3.64$).

\paragraph{Sample.} Requiring at least three, five or seven years per firm,
using only firms observed in every fiscal year 2012--2024 (3{,}367
firm-years), and dropping the market capitalisations based on weighted-average
share counts leave the results intact. Dropping every firm-year with an
imputed balance-sheet value reduces the sample to 3{,}820 firm-years, mostly
because total liabilities are often computed from assets and equity; length
stays significant ($+0.078$, $t = +4.87$), but LitDensity is no longer
($-1.761$, $t = -1.05$). The LitDensity result therefore depends partly on
firm-years whose leverage is constructed rather than reported. For 626 of
the dropped firm-years the constructed liabilities also contain
non-controlling interests, because those firms report parent-only equity
(Section~\ref{sec:xbrl_tags}), so this check also removes that measurement
error.

\paragraph{Counts instead of ratios.} With $\ln(1+n^{\text{unc}})$ and
$\ln(1+n^{\text{lit}})$ next to $\ln(\text{Words}^{1A})$, no ratio enters
the model. The count-based specification uses 5,855 observations rather than
5,782 because the raw counts remain defined for firm-years with very short or
poorly parsed Item~1A sections, while the density ratios are set to missing
when the section is too short or the parsing fails. In this specification,
the length coefficient is $-0.050$ ($t = -1.76$), the uncertainty count is
$+0.098$ ($t = +2.47$), and the litigious count is essentially zero
($-0.001$, $t = -0.09$). The row therefore does not show evidence that the
litigious count is associated with volatility; it shows that the ratio form
matters for the significance of the text variable. To compare the
specifications on identical observations, the density-based and count-based
models need to be re-estimated on the same sample rather than read as direct
replications of one another.

\paragraph{Timing.} Using the text of the previous year's 10-K instead of
the current one leaves the length coefficient unchanged ($+0.057$) and
LitDensity weaker ($-2.449$, $t = -1.95$). When the level of year $t-1$ and
the change from $t-1$ to $t$ enter together, the levels carry the
association and the changes are insignificant. The text variables
therefore act as slowly moving firm characteristics; the association is not
driven by the revisions made in the current filing.

\paragraph{Before and after 2020.} Interacting the three text variables with
a dummy for fiscal years from 2020 shows no significant change after 2019:
the interaction terms have $t$-statistics of $+1.42$ (length), $+1.02$
(UncDensity) and $-1.09$ (LitDensity). In this specification the pre-2020
LitDensity coefficient alone is not significant ($t = -1.47$); the pooled
estimate is more precise than either sub-period.

\paragraph{Inference with few year clusters.} Table~\ref{tab:inference}
compares $p$-values. The wild cluster bootstrap by fiscal year gives
$p = 0.001$ for length and $p = 0.005$ for LitDensity in the industry
fixed-effects model, close to the two-way clustered values; with one-way
firm clustering LitDensity has $p = 0.028$. The conclusions do not depend on
the clustering choice.

\section{Firm fixed effects}
\label{sec:robustness_firmfe}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The headline models compare firms within an industry and year. Replacing the
industry effects by firm effects asks a different question: whether a firm
whose Item~1A becomes longer or denser than usual has higher volatility
than usual. Table~\ref{tab:firm_fe} reports the answer, and
Figure~\ref{fig:coef_fe} compares the coefficients.

\input{tables/firm_fe}

Under firm fixed effects the length coefficient falls from $+0.058$ to
$+0.009$ ($t = +0.45$), and no density coefficient is significant
(LitDensity $-3.132$, $t = -1.02$). Adding the lagged dependent variable,
whose coefficient is biased under firm fixed effects \parencite{nickell1981},
changes nothing (column 3). The associations in Chapter~\ref{ch:results} are
therefore between-firm patterns: firms with longer and more litigious
risk-factor sections differ systematically in subsequent volatility from
otherwise similar firms in the same industry and year, but year-to-year
changes in a firm's own section do not track changes in its volatility. Two
features of the data limit what the firm fixed-effects models can show.
First, most of the variation in the text variables is between firms: only
18\% of the variance of $\ln(\text{Words}^{1A})$ and 19\% of that of
LitDensity is within firms (Table~\ref{tab:variance}), so the within
estimates are imprecise; the confidence interval of the within-firm
LitDensity coefficient contains the between-firm estimate. Second, Item~1A
changes slowly from year to year \parencite{brown2011, cazier2021}, which is
consistent with this variance split.

\section{The omission measure (H3)}
\label{sec:robustness_silence}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

H3 is not supported in Chapter~\ref{ch:results}. This section examines the
measure itself, to show that the null result does not come from a single
construction choice, and reports the checks that address the criticisms of
the earlier version of the measure. Table~\ref{tab:silence_robust} reports
the coefficients and Table~\ref{tab:silence_diag} the diagnostics.

\input{tables/silence_robust}
\input{tables/silence_diag}

\paragraph{Length.} The omission gap is strongly related to length:
$r = -0.915$ with $\ln(\text{Words}^{1A})$ in the H3 sample of 4{,}659
firm-years (Figure~\ref{fig:omission_length}). A longer section mechanically
covers more of the peer vocabulary. Three versions remove this link in
different ways. The length-invariant gap computed from blocks of 3{,}000
tokens has a correlation of $+0.424$ with length instead (the remaining
relation runs through how varied a section's vocabulary is); the residual
from a regression of the gap on length within each industry-year cell is
uncorrelated with length by construction; and adding $\ln(\text{Words}^{\text{MD\&A}})$
and the square of $\ln(\text{Words}^{1A})$ allows for a non-linear length
effect. None of these versions is significant under either fixed-effect
structure.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{img/fig_omission_length.pdf}
\caption{Omission gap against $\ln(\text{Words}^{1A})$, one point per
firm-year of the 30-day H3 sample, with the OLS fit.}
\label{fig:omission_length}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Sources of variation.} Under firm fixed effects the gap varies
only from year to year. The decomposition in Table~\ref{tab:silence_diag}
attributes 91\% of the variance of the year-on-year change to changes in the
firm's own text, 6\% to changes in the peers' texts and 3\% to entering and
leaving peers. A fixed vocabulary for all years gives a gap with the same
(insignificant) coefficients. The within-firm variation of the gap is
therefore mostly a revision of the firm's own section, and only 13\% of its
variance is within firms (Table~\ref{tab:variance}).

\paragraph{Peer definition.} Peers defined by today's SIC code, and only
cells with at least 20 peers (3{,}262 firm-years; Table~\ref{tab:h3_cells} in
Appendix~\ref{app:variables} lists the industries, which are dominated by
business services, utilities, chemicals and pharmaceuticals, machinery and
electronics), give the same result.

\paragraph{Ranks and tails.} The decile rank of the gap within its
industry-year cell and a dummy for the top decile (with the bottom decile
controlled) are insignificant. These versions need cells with at least ten
firms, which reduces the sample from 4{,}659 to 3{,}667 firm-years; 992
firm-years lie in smaller cells.

\paragraph{Timing.} The gap of the previous fiscal year, which is known long
before the current filing and cannot reflect conditions at that filing, has a
positive coefficient that is significant at the 5\% level under firm fixed
effects ($+0.726$, $t = +1.97$) but not under industry effects
($t = +1.37$). As one of many checks, and without a significant
contemporaneous counterpart, this does not change the conclusion.

\paragraph{Placebo.} Firms were reassigned at random to industry cells within
each fiscal year, keeping the cell sizes, the gap was recomputed and the
firm fixed-effects model re-estimated, 1{,}000 times. The observed
$t$-statistic of $+0.70$ lies in the middle of the placebo distribution: the
empirical two-sided $p$-value is 0.466, and 2.4\% of the
placebo $t$-statistics exceed 1.96 (Figure~\ref{fig:placebo}). The real peer
groups carry no more information about volatility than random ones.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{img/fig_placebo.pdf}
\caption{Distribution of the omission-gap $t$-statistic in 1{,}000 placebo
assignments of firms to industry cells (firm and year fixed effects, 30-day
window), with the observed value.}
\label{fig:placebo}
\end{figure}

\paragraph{Summary for H3.} Across the eleven specifications and both
fixed-effect
structures, the omission coefficient is significant in one (the prior-year
gap under firm fixed effects, at the 5\% level without a multiple-testing
adjustment). The verdict of Section~\ref{sec:silence_results} stands: H3 is
not supported.

\section{Persistence and unit roots}
\label{sec:robustness_stationarity}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

With at most 15 annual observations per firm, the main risk of a dynamic
panel is the Nickell bias discussed in Section~\ref{sec:estimation}, not a
spurious regression. For completeness, Table~\ref{tab:stationarity} reports
two panel unit-root tests designed for short panels. Every variable is first
demeaned by fiscal year. The Harris--Tzavalis test \parencite{harris1999}
uses the balanced panel of firms observed in all years 2012--2024 and tests
the null that every firm's series has a unit root against a common
autoregressive coefficient below one; its $p$-values are simulated for the
actual number of firms and years. The Im--Pesaran--Shin test \parencite{ips2003}
averages firm-level augmented Dickey--Fuller statistics on the unbalanced
panel; its null is that all firms have a unit root, and a rejection means
that at least some firms' series are stationary, not that all are.

\input{tables/stationarity}

Both tests reject the unit-root null for volatility, the text variables and
most controls. Two variables are exceptions. For leverage the
Harris--Tzavalis test does not reject ($\hat\rho = 0.772$, $p = 0.592$),
while the Im--Pesaran--Shin test does; for the omission gap neither test
rejects at the 5\% level ($\hat\rho = 0.758$, $p = 0.257$; IPS $p = 0.099$). Leverage is
a control, and the omission gap enters only the H3 models, whose coefficient
is insignificant. A ratio bounded between zero and one cannot follow a random
walk forever, but over 13 years it can be persistent enough that these tests
lack power; the results are therefore read as showing high persistence for
these two variables rather than as proof of stationarity.
