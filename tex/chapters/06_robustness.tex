% =============================================================================
\chapter{Robustness}
\label{ch:robustness}
% =============================================================================

This chapter checks that the headline results of
Chapter~\ref{ch:results} survive plausible variations in the
specification. Section~\ref{sec:robustness_horizons} reviews the
multi-horizon evidence already documented in
Section~\ref{sec:horizons_results} from a robustness angle.
Section~\ref{sec:robustness_subperiods} re-estimates the H1+H3
decomposition specification on the pre-COVID and COVID-era
sub-samples. Section~\ref{sec:robustness_firmfe} replaces industry
fixed effects with firm fixed effects, which absorbs all
time-invariant firm-level heterogeneity and isolates the
within-firm year-to-year variation in the three text variables.
Section~\ref{sec:robustness_failed} closes the chapter with a
transparent account of the seven alternative H3 candidates that
were tested and rejected before settling on the LM-list
decomposition.

\section{Alternative post-filing windows}
\label{sec:robustness_horizons}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The headline specification measures post-filing volatility over a
30-day window. One month is short enough to capture the bulk of the
post-filing reaction documented by \textcite{loughran2011} but long
enough that the volatility estimate is built on roughly 21~trading
days of returns. To check the choice of window we re-estimate the
H1+H3 specification of equation~\ref{eq:decomposition_model} at
five additional horizons (5, 10, 90, 180, and 365~days) and inspect
the resulting series of coefficients. Table~\ref{tab:horizons}
reports all six columns side by side; the discussion in
Section~\ref{sec:horizons_results} is repeated here from a
robustness angle.

The pattern is unambiguous in three respects. First, all three text
coefficients keep the predicted sign at every horizon---positive
for $\ln(\text{Words}^{1A})$, negative for LitDensity, and
statistically indistinguishable from zero for UncDensity---so
neither H1 nor H3 reverses for any plausible choice of window.
Second, the LitDensity coefficient is significant at the 5\% level
at every horizon, with $|t|$ between $2.28$ (5~days) and $4.44$
(90~days). Third, the UncDensity coefficient never approaches
statistical significance, with $|t| \leq 1$ at all windows except
5~days. The 30-day choice for the headline specification is
therefore not a window in which the results happen to be
sharpest---they are sharp at every horizon the data admit.

Both coefficient series also decline approximately monotonically
in $h$, which is the empirical signature of an event-driven signal
that is incorporated into prices over weeks rather than years
\parencite{loughran2011, tetlock2007}. The thesis does not fit a
formal exponential decay because the coefficient series is
short and the across-horizon error terms are correlated by the
sharing of underlying daily returns, which would understate the
bootstrap CI on any half-life estimate. The qualitative attenuation
is nonetheless visible by inspection of Table~\ref{tab:horizons}.

\section{Pre-COVID and COVID-era sub-samples}
\label{sec:robustness_subperiods}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The 2020 fiscal year and its aftermath were a structural shock for
both equity volatility and corporate disclosure: average annualised
volatility roughly doubled in the spring of 2020 and Item~1A
sections grew systematically longer as firms added pandemic and
supply-chain language. To check that the headline results are not
driven by either the pre-COVID baseline or the COVID-era spike, we
re-estimate the H1+H3 decomposition specification
(equation~\ref{eq:decomposition_model}) separately on the two
sub-periods. Filings with fiscal year $\leq 2019$ form the pre-COVID
sample (3{,}243~firm-years) and filings with fiscal year $\geq 2020$
form the COVID-era sample (2{,}448~firm-years). All other features
of the specification are unchanged. Table~\ref{tab:subperiod}
reports the result.

\input{tables/subperiods}

The two active text channels are present in both sub-periods with
the predicted signs and at the same order of magnitude. Length is
significant at the 1\% level in both ($\beta = +0.103$, $t = +5.45$
pre-COVID; $\beta = +0.172$, $t = +7.01$ COVID era). LitDensity is
negative and significant in both ($\beta = -5.49$, $t = -2.52$
pre-COVID; $\beta = -9.88$, $t = -3.86$ COVID era). UncDensity is
statistically indistinguishable from zero in both samples,
consistent with the headline finding that uncertainty wording adds
no explanatory power once length and litigious wording are
controlled for. The two active coefficients (length and LitDensity)
are larger in absolute value in the COVID-era sample, consistent
with the higher overall level of volatility in that period giving
more scale to the disclosure signal, but the qualitative pattern is
identical. The headline finding is not a COVID artefact.

\section{Within-firm identification: firm fixed effects}
\label{sec:robustness_firmfe}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The headline specification absorbs two-digit SIC industry and
fiscal-year fixed effects. Coefficients are therefore identified
from variation \emph{within an industry-year cell}, which removes
the most plausible confounders (industry-level differences in
risk vocabulary and macroeconomic time effects) but still allows
permanent firm-level traits to drive the result. To rule that out
we replace the industry fixed effects with firm fixed effects, so
that identification comes only from \emph{within-firm} year-to-year
variation in the three text variables. Table~\ref{tab:firm_fe}
reports the result at the 30-day horizon.

\input{tables/firm_fe}

The volume channel survives the firmer specification:
$\hat{\beta}^{\text{len}} = +0.084$ ($t = +3.31$, $p < 0.001$).
A within-firm one-standard-deviation rise in
$\ln(\text{Words}^{1A})$ still predicts a meaningful rise in
post-filing volatility, so the H1 result is not a cross-sectional
artefact---firms that grow their Item~1A from year to year do
experience higher subsequent volatility.

Both LM sub-densities, by contrast, are absorbed by firm fixed
effects: $\hat{\beta}^{\text{unc}} = -1.46$ ($t = -0.55$) and
$\hat{\beta}^{\text{lit}} = +0.96$ ($t = +0.35$). The interpretation
is straightforward and economically informative. The composition of
an Item~1A's risk wording---both the uncertainty share and the
litigious share---has very little within-firm variation across
years, because the section is largely recycled from one filing to
the next \parencite{cazier2021} and the legal advisers who shape
its cautionary language change slowly. A specification that throws
away the cross-sectional variation has therefore almost no
remaining variance in either density to identify a coefficient
from.

This pattern is consistent with the production-side reading of
litigious language given by
\textcite{nelson2007,cazier2021,beatty2019}: cautionary boilerplate
is a quasi-permanent firm-level disclosure-style trait that proxies
for an institutionalised, legally supervised compliance function.
It is precisely such a permanent firm trait that would be
cross-sectionally informative but within-firm null.

A single thesis cannot adjudicate between the two readings of
LitDensity that the data are consistent with: (i)~it captures a
genuine causal between-firm effect of legal-style boilerplate on
perceived risk, or (ii)~it correlates with an unobserved
firm-level trait (e.g.\ governance quality, legal-counsel
seniority, board composition) that itself drives volatility. The
firm-FE result rules out the second reading only as a within-firm
driver. The thesis reports both findings transparently and reads
the headline result as a between-firm regularity that aligns with
the \textcite{nelson2007,cazier2021,beatty2019} interpretation,
without claiming a within-firm causal channel.

\paragraph{Bottom line.} Across alternative horizons and
sub-periods, the qualitative picture of
Chapter~\ref{ch:results} is preserved: longer risk-factor sections
predict higher post-filing volatility, and---wherever
cross-sectional variation in disclosure style is
available---denser litigious-list sections predict lower
post-filing volatility, while denser uncertainty-list sections do
not. H1 clears all robustness checks including firm-FE; H2 and H3
clear the cross-sectional and sub-period checks but are absorbed
by firm-FE because both the composite RiskDensity and its two LM
sub-components are quasi-permanent firm-level traits.

\section{What did not work: alternative H3 candidates}
\label{sec:robustness_failed}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

In the spirit of full disclosure, this section documents the
seven text-based H3 candidates that were estimated on the same
panel and rejected before the LM-list decomposition was adopted.
All seven specifications add a third Item~1A or MD\&A text
variable to the H1+H2 joint specification of
equation~\ref{eq:joint_model} and ask whether it adds incremental
explanatory power for $\ln \sigma_{i,t+1}^{[30]}$.

\paragraph{Year-on-year textual similarity (Item~1A).} A TF--IDF
cosine similarity between consecutive years' Item~1A sections, in
the spirit of \textcite{brown2011}'s MD\&A modification score, was
estimated as a measure of disclosure ``staleness''. The coefficient
was not statistically distinguishable from zero in the joint
specification, with the predicted sign nonetheless reversing across
horizons.

\paragraph{MD\&A LM-Negativity.} The share of LM-Negative words in
the Management's Discussion and Analysis section was estimated as a
functionally distinct sentiment proxy. It did not add incremental
explanatory power once Item~1A length was controlled for.

\paragraph{Year-on-year change in Item~1A length ($\Delta$Words).}
The first difference of $\ln(\text{Words}^{1A})$, motivated by the
\textcite{lyle2023} ``changes'' framing, was statistically
indistinguishable from zero in the joint specification.

\paragraph{FinBERT-tone (four functional forms).} A pretrained
FinBERT-tone classifier (\texttt{yiyanghkust/finbert-tone}) was
run on every Item~1A in the panel. Four candidate signals were
constructed from the per-sentence tone probabilities and aggregated
to the section level: (i)~$P(\text{negative})$, (ii)~the signed
difference $P(\text{positive}) - P(\text{negative})$, (iii)~the
share of neutral sentences $P(\text{neutral})$, and (iv)~the
``intensity'' measure $1 - P(\text{neutral})$. None of the four
adds statistically significant explanatory power to the joint
specification at the 30-day horizon, with $|t| < 1$ in all
four cases. The detailed null results are not tabulated in the
interest of brevity, but the FinBERT scoring pipeline
(\texttt{scripts/finbert\_score\_mac.py}) and its per-firm output
are preserved in the project repository for future replication.

The negative results have an intuitive reading. The TF--IDF and
FinBERT signals capture firm-specific narrative changes whose
informational content is, on this panel, dominated by the much
stronger length signal of H1. The LM-list decomposition succeeds
where these alternatives fail because it identifies a structural
composition of the H2 specificity effect rather than an additional
firm-specific signal that competes with H1.
