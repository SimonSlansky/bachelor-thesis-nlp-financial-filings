% =============================================================================
\chapter{Methodology}
\label{ch:methodology}
% =============================================================================

This chapter sets out the regression specifications for the three
hypotheses. All tests regress post-filing realised volatility on
characteristics of the Item~1A risk-factor section, a common vector of
controls known when the 10-K is filed, and fixed effects.
Section~\ref{sec:outcome} defines the dependent variable and the timing of
all variables; Section~\ref{sec:controls} lists the controls, which are the
same in every specification so that the text coefficients can be compared
across hypotheses. Sections~\ref{sec:h1} and~\ref{sec:h2} describe the joint
test of the volume channel (H1) and the composition channel (H2), which are
estimated together because the density measure for H2 has section length in
its denominator. Section~\ref{sec:h3} introduces the peer-relative omission
gap for H3 and the decision rule used to evaluate it, and
Section~\ref{sec:estimation} covers estimation and inference.


% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Outcome variable and timing}
\label{sec:outcome}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The dependent variable is the natural logarithm of annualised post-filing
stock-return volatility measured over a window of $h$~calendar days:
\begin{equation}
\ln \sigma_{i,t+1}^{[h]} \;=\; \ln\!\left(
\sqrt{252}\cdot
\operatorname{sd}\!\bigl(r_{i,d}\bigr)_{d \in W_{i,t}^{[h]}}
\right),
\label{eq:vol}
\end{equation}
where $r_{i,d} = \ln(P_{i,d}/P_{i,d-1})$ is the daily log return of firm~$i$
computed from dividend- and split-adjusted closing prices, and
$W_{i,t}^{[h]}$ is the post-filing window that belongs to the 10-K for fiscal
year~$t$. The subscript $t+1$ marks that the window lies after the filing;
it is not the next fiscal year. Day~0 is the first trading day on or after
the filing date. The window begins two trading days after day~0, so the
market reaction on the filing day and the next day is left out, and it then
runs for $h$~calendar days. Only returns between two prices inside the
window are used. A window needs a minimum number of daily returns (3 for
$h = 5$, 5 for $h = 10$, 14 for $h = 30$, 44, 89 and 199 for the longer
windows); with fewer, or a zero standard deviation, the volatility is
missing. A window whose nominal end lies after the last trading day of the
price data (30~October 2026) is also missing rather than truncated; with
this cut-off every fiscal-year~2024 filing has a complete 365-day window
(Section~\ref{sec:sample_selection}).

Figure~\ref{fig:timeline} shows the timing. Every control and every text
variable is measured from information that is public at day~0: the
accounting variables and the text come from the same 10-K, and the
price-based controls use windows that end on the last trading day before
the filing date.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{img/fig_timeline.pdf}
\caption{Timing of the variables for the 10-K of fiscal year $t$ (not to
scale). The outcome window starts two trading days after day~0 and lasts
$h$ calendar days; pre-filing volatility uses the 60 trading days, and the
past return and the liquidity rule the 252 trading days, that end on the
last trading day before the filing date.}
\label{fig:timeline}
\end{figure}

A coefficient $\beta$ on a regressor $x$ implies a change of
$100(e^{\beta\Delta x}-1)\%$ in volatility for a change $\Delta x$. The
headline horizon is $h = 30$ calendar days, chosen to keep the outcome close
to the filing and limit exposure to later information events. Prior studies
linking 10-K text to subsequent stock-return volatility have used
approximately one-year post-filing windows \parencite{campbell2014,
kogan2009, loughran2011}. The 365-day window is reported alongside the
headline result, while $h \in \{5, 10, 90, 180\}$ are examined as robustness
checks.

\section{Control variables}
\label{sec:controls}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Every regression for H1, H2 and H3 includes the same vector
$\mathbf{X}_{i,t}$ of nine controls, all known at day~0:
\begin{itemize}
  \item pre-filing volatility $\ln\sigma^{\mathrm{pre}}_{i,t}$: the log of the
    annualised standard deviation of the 60 daily returns ending on the last
    trading day before the filing date;
  \item firm size $\ln(\text{Market cap})$: the as-traded closing price on the
    last trading day before filing times the shares outstanding reported in
    the same 10-K;
  \item book-to-market: book equity attributable to the parent over market
    capitalisation;
  \item the past 12-month log return, over the 252 trading days before filing;
  \item leverage (total liabilities over total assets), return on assets
    (consolidated net income over total assets), asset growth, the current
    ratio, and operating cash flow over total assets.
\end{itemize}
Pre-filing volatility is a key control because stock-return volatility is
persistent \parencite{kogan2009}, and disclosure characteristics may reflect
conditions already visible before filing. Firm size, book-to-market, past
returns and leverage are also used in prior risk-disclosure regressions
\parencite{campbell2014}, although the variable definitions differ. The
remaining accounting ratios control for profitability, growth, liquidity and
operating cash flow. Operating cash flow over assets enters the main
specifications rather than serving as a robustness check.

The earlier version of this model used the volatility of the same-length
window after the previous year's 10-K as the dynamic control. Once
pre-filing volatility is included, that lag adds nothing (its coefficient is
$+0.031$ with $t=+0.69$ in the industry fixed-effects model;
Table~\ref{tab:firm_fe} reports the firm fixed-effects version), so it is
reported only as a robustness check. Each firm's first fiscal year is not in
the sample, because asset growth needs the previous year's total assets.
Financial ratios, book-to-market and the past return are winsorised at the
1st and 99th percentiles of each regression sample; market capitalisation and
pre-filing volatility enter in logs and are not winsorised.

\section{H1 --- Item~1A length and disclosure volume}
\label{sec:h1}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Following \textcite{campbell2014}, disclosure volume is measured as the
logarithm of the Item~1A word count. Campbell et al.\ find that firms with
longer risk-factor sections have higher subsequent market-based risk
measures, consistent with the volume of risk discussion carrying information
about firm risk. The volume variable is
\begin{equation}
\text{Words}^{1A}_{i,t} \;=\; \text{number of tokens in Item~1A of firm
$i$'s fiscal-year-$t$ 10-K},
\end{equation}
where a token is a run of at least two letters in the lowercased text
(Section~\ref{ssec:text_postprocessing}). The cleaning removes running
page headers, page numbers and numeric table rows but keeps the text of
layout tables and footnote markers, so the count is close to, but not
exactly, the narrative content. The median Item~1A in the estimation sample
has 8{,}700 tokens (Table~\ref{tab:summary}).

\paragraph{H1.} \emph{Holding the composition of Item~1A and the controls
fixed, a longer Item~1A is associated with higher post-filing volatility}:
$\beta^{\text{len}} > 0$ in
\begin{multline}
\ln \sigma_{i,t+1}^{[h]} \;=\; \alpha
\;+\; \boldsymbol{\gamma}' \mathbf{X}_{i,t}
\;+\; \beta^{\text{len}} \ln(\text{Words}^{1A}_{i,t}) \\
\;+\; \beta^{\text{dens}} \,\text{RiskDensity}_{i,t}
\;+\; \mu_{j(i,t)} \;+\; \tau_t
\;+\; \varepsilon_{i,t},
\label{eq:joint_model}
\end{multline}
where $\mu_j$ is a fixed effect for the two-digit SIC industry of the firm at
the filing date, $\tau_t$ a fiscal-year fixed effect, and RiskDensity is
defined in Section~\ref{sec:h2}. Length and density enter together because
the density has length in its denominator.


% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{H2 --- Risk-word density}
\label{sec:h2}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

H2 concerns the composition of Item~1A at a given length: the share of words
from the uncertainty and litigious dictionaries of \textcite{loughran2011},
which were built for financial reporting,
\begin{equation}
\text{RiskDensity}_{i,t} \;=\; \frac{n^{\text{unc}}_{i,t} + n^{\text{lit}}_{i,t}}
{\text{Words}^{1A}_{i,t}},
\label{eq:risk_density}
\end{equation}
where $n^{\text{unc}}$ and $n^{\text{lit}}$ count the tokens of Item~1A that
belong to each list. The two lists share no word in the dictionary version
used (Section~\ref{ssec:text_postprocessing}), so no token is counted twice.

The sign of the association is not determined by theory, and H2 is therefore
stated two-sided. Two channels pull in opposite directions.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Risk-content channel.} Uncertainty and legal-exposure words name
    risks. At a given length, a section dense in such words discusses more
    risk, and if the discussion is informative about firm risk
    \parencite{campbell2014, kravet2013}, density should be associated with
    \emph{higher} volatility.
  \item \emph{Boilerplate channel.} Much of this vocabulary is standard
    cautionary language that firms repeat across years and that applies to
    any firm \parencite{dyer2017, cazier2021}. At a given length, a section
    dense in standard risk terms leaves less room for firm-specific
    discussion, which is the kind of risk disclosure that investors find
    informative \parencite{hope2016}. Under this channel density is
    associated with \emph{lower} volatility.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{H2.} \emph{Holding length and the controls fixed, RiskDensity is
associated with post-filing volatility}: $\beta^{\text{dens}} \neq 0$ in
equation~\eqref{eq:joint_model}. The sign identifies the dominating channel.
Density is a proxy for composition, not a direct measure of specificity in
the sense of \textcite{hope2016}: words such as ``litigation'' or
``investigation'' can describe very firm-specific circumstances.

\paragraph{Sub-list decomposition.} The second stage replaces RiskDensity by
its two components, $\text{UncDensity} = n^{\text{unc}}/\text{Words}^{1A}$ and
$\text{LitDensity} = n^{\text{lit}}/\text{Words}^{1A}$:
\begin{multline}
\ln \sigma_{i,t+1}^{[h]} \;=\; \alpha
\;+\; \boldsymbol{\gamma}' \mathbf{X}_{i,t}
\;+\; \beta^{\text{len}} \ln(\text{Words}^{1A}_{i,t}) \\
\;+\; \beta^{\text{unc}} \,\text{UncDensity}_{i,t}
\;+\; \beta^{\text{lit}} \,\text{LitDensity}_{i,t}
\;+\; \mu_{j(i,t)} \;+\; \tau_t
\;+\; \varepsilon_{i,t}.
\label{eq:decomposition_model}
\end{multline}
The uncertainty list covers ambiguity and incomplete knowledge of outcomes;
the litigious list covers legal exposure, regulation, investigations and
disputes \parencite{loughran2011}. A Wald test of
$\beta^{\text{unc}} = \beta^{\text{lit}}$ asks whether the two behave
differently.

\paragraph{Ratio regressors.} Densities are ratios with length in the
denominator, and length is also a regressor. A regression on ratios can
produce associations that come from the denominator alone
\parencite{kronmal1993}. Two features limit this. First, length is in the
model, so the density coefficient is estimated at a given length. Second,
a count specification replaces the densities by $\ln(1+n^{\text{unc}})$ and
$\ln(1+n^{\text{lit}})$ next to $\ln(\text{Words}^{1A})$, which uses no
ratio at all (Section~\ref{sec:robustness_h12}).

\section{H3 --- Peer-relative omission gap}
\label{sec:h3}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

H3 asks whether what a firm \emph{leaves out} of Item~1A relative to its
industry peers is associated with subsequent volatility, beyond length and
density. The \emph{omission gap} is the measure; \emph{strategic silence} is
one interpretation of it, namely that a firm stays quiet on risks its peers
disclose. Non-strategic explanations predict the same correlation: a firm
may not face the risk, may describe it in different words, or may have a
shorter section for reasons unrelated to risk.

\paragraph{Construction.} For each fiscal year $t$, a TF--IDF model is fitted
on the Item~1A texts filed before the first fiscal-year-$t$ filing in the
panel, so that vocabulary and weights are known at every focal filing
date (fiscal year 2010 has no earlier texts and therefore no omission gap).
The settings are those of \texttt{scikit-learn}: lowercased text, tokens of at
least two letters or digits, the library's English stop-word list removed,
single words only, terms in at least five and at most 95\% of the training
documents, raw term counts, smoothed inverse document frequency, and rows
normalised to unit length. Let $\mathbf{x}_{i,t}$ be the TF--IDF row of firm
$i$'s fiscal-year-$t$ Item~1A. The peers of a focal filing are the other
panel firms in the same two-digit SIC industry (at the filing date); for each
peer the most recent Item~1A of fiscal year $t$ or $t-1$ filed strictly
before the focal filing is used, so no later information enters. With
$P_{i,t}$ this peer set,
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{p}_{i,t} = \sum_{k \in P_{i,t}} \mathbf{x}_{k},
\qquad
\textsc{Omission}^{1A}_{i,t} \;=\; 1 \;-\;
  \frac{\sum_{w : x_{i,t,w} > 0}\, p_{i,t,w}}{\sum_{w}\, p_{i,t,w}},
\label{eq:omission}
\end{equation}
the share of peer TF--IDF mass on terms the focal firm does not use. It lies
in $[0,1]$. Filings with fewer than five peers get no value.

\paragraph{Length and the sources of variation.} Omission is mechanically
related to length, because a longer section uses more of the peer
vocabulary; in the H3 sample the correlation with $\ln(\text{Words}^{1A})$ is
$-0.915$ (Table~\ref{tab:silence_diag}). Three variants address this and
the question of where its variation comes from:
\begin{itemize}
  \item a \emph{length-invariant} omission gap: for each filing, 20 random
    contiguous blocks of 3{,}000 tokens are drawn from its Item~1A (fixed
    seed), the gap is computed with each block in place of the full text,
    and the 20 values are averaged; sections shorter than 3{,}000 tokens use
    the whole text;
  \item a \emph{pooled-vocabulary} gap, with one TF--IDF model fitted on all
    years, which removes changes in the vocabulary over time (it uses later
    documents and is a diagnostic only);
  \item a decomposition of the year-on-year change of the gap into the change
    of the firm's own text (peers held at $t-1$), the change of the peers'
    texts, and the change in peer composition.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Specification.} The H3 regression adds the omission gap to
equation~\eqref{eq:joint_model}:
\begin{multline}
\ln \sigma_{i,t+1}^{[h]} \;=\; \alpha
\;+\; \boldsymbol{\gamma}' \mathbf{X}_{i,t}
\;+\; \beta^{\text{len}} \ln(\text{Words}^{1A}_{i,t})
\;+\; \beta^{\text{dens}} \,\text{RiskDensity}_{i,t} \\
\;+\; \beta^{\text{om}} \,\textsc{Omission}^{1A}_{i,t}
\;+\; \mu \;+\; \tau_t
\;+\; \varepsilon_{i,t},
\label{eq:silence_model}
\end{multline}
estimated with industry fixed effects ($\mu = \mu_j$) and with firm fixed
effects ($\mu = \mu_i$). The controls are the common vector of
Section~\ref{sec:controls}, so the H3 model differs from the H1/H2 model
only by the omission gap.

\paragraph{H3 and the decision rule.} \emph{Holding length, density and the
controls fixed, a larger omission gap is associated with higher post-filing
volatility}: $\beta^{\text{om}} > 0$. The evidence is judged by a fixed rule,
recorded in the project log before the models were re-estimated:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item The family of tests is $h \in \{5, 10, 30, 90, 180\}$ days under both
    fixed-effect structures (ten two-sided tests); $p$-values are adjusted
    with the Holm step-down procedure \parencite{holm1979} at $\alpha = 0.05$.
  \item The primary design is $h = 30$ days. Under a given fixed-effect
    structure there is \emph{positive evidence} if $\beta^{\text{om}} > 0$
    with Holm-adjusted $p < 0.05$ \emph{and} the length-invariant gap has a
    positive coefficient with $p < 0.05$ in the same specification;
    \emph{negative evidence} is the mirror image.
  \item H3 is \emph{supported} with positive evidence under both structures,
    \emph{partially supported} with positive evidence under one and no
    negative evidence under the other, \emph{contradicted} with only negative
    evidence, and \emph{not supported} otherwise. The two fixed-effect
    structures are treated symmetrically.
\end{enumerate}
The placebo test, the decomposition and the other robustness checks are
reported but do not change the verdict.

\section{Estimation and inference}
\label{sec:estimation}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

All regressions are estimated by ordinary least squares with the fixed
effects absorbed (\texttt{linearmodels}). With industry and year fixed
effects, the coefficients use variation \emph{between} firms within an
industry-year cell. Firm fixed effects are used as a robustness check
(Section~\ref{sec:robustness_firmfe}); they use only year-to-year
variation \emph{within} a firm. The two answer different questions, and a
coefficient estimated under industry fixed effects is not evidence of a
within-firm relation. The analysis is predictive, not causal: the
coefficients measure conditional associations between the disclosure and
subsequent volatility, as in \textcite{campbell2014}.

\paragraph{Fiscal-year effects.} The year fixed effects absorb shocks common
to all firms of a fiscal year. Because fiscal years end in different months,
the filings of one fiscal year are spread over more than a year of calendar
time (Section~\ref{sec:xbrl_tags}), so a fiscal-year effect does not remove
calendar-time market shocks: the volatility spike of spring 2020, for
example, falls in the post-filing windows of firms with different fiscal-year
labels and in only part of the windows within one label.
Four robustness checks address this:
the market (SPY) volatility over the same window as a control, filing-month
$\times$ fiscal-year fixed effects, fixed effects for the calendar month of
the filing in place of the fiscal-year effects, and the sample of firms with
a December fiscal-year end, for which fiscal and calendar time coincide. The
idiosyncratic volatility (the residual standard deviation of a market-model
regression inside the window) is used as a further dependent variable.

\paragraph{Anticipated volatility.} Item~1A is written when management already
knows current conditions, so expected volatility could shape the wording.
Pre-filing volatility, which is public at filing, captures the part of this
that is visible in prices. It does not rule out that management anticipates
future volatility beyond it, so all results are read as associations.

\paragraph{Standard errors.} Standard errors are clustered two-way by firm and
fiscal year \parencite{petersen2009, cameron2011robust}. Firm clusters allow
for persistent firm-specific shocks that make a firm's residuals correlated
over years; year clusters allow for shocks common to all firms in a fiscal
year, such as the 2020 market turmoil. With 15 fiscal years the year
dimension has few clusters, and cluster-robust tests can then over-reject
\parencite{cameron2008, cameron2015}. For the text coefficients we therefore
also report $p$-values from a wild cluster bootstrap by fiscal year with
Webb weights and 9{,}999 draws \parencite{webb2023}, and from one-way firm
clustering.

\paragraph{Dynamics and Nickell bias.} With firm fixed effects and at most 15
years per firm, a lagged dependent variable is correlated with the
within-transformed error, and its coefficient is biased towards zero by an
amount of order $1/T$ \parencite{nickell1981}; the bias can carry over to
the other coefficients. The firm fixed-effects models therefore use
pre-filing volatility, which is measured before the window and is not a lag
of the dependent variable, as the dynamic control, and report the version
with the lagged dependent variable only for comparison. A related concern,
spurious regression from integrated series, is less relevant with $T \leq 15$
and large $N$, but Section~\ref{sec:robustness_stationarity} reports panel
unit-root tests designed for this case.

\paragraph{Outliers.} Accounting ratios, book-to-market and the past return
are winsorised as described in Section~\ref{sec:controls}. The text variables
are not winsorised: $\ln(\text{Words}^{1A})$ ranges from 7.41 to 10.48
between the 1st and 99th percentiles, and RiskDensity has a maximum of
0.0772 (Table~\ref{tab:summary}).

\paragraph{Sample.} The 30-day estimation sample has 5{,}782 firm-years of
512 firms; the same firm-years enter the 365-day models. Table~\ref{tab:attrition}
in Appendix~\ref{app:variables} traces the sample from the SEC ticker list
to this sample.

\paragraph{Limitation: survivorship and look-ahead.} The universe is the
first 1{,}000 rows of the SEC ticker list as stored in October 2026, which is
ordered by approximately current size. Firms that were delisted, acquired or
failed during 2010--2024 are absent, and firms that are large today are
over-represented in early years: the panel grows from 152 firm-years in
fiscal year 2010 to over 500 from 2020 (Table~\ref{tab:text_coverage}).
Firms in distress are likely to be riskier, so the results describe large
surviving firms. The sample rules themselves look only backwards: the
liquidity requirement (Section~\ref{sec:sample_selection}) uses prices
before the filing.
