% =============================================================================
\chapter{Methodology}
\label{ch:methodology}
% =============================================================================

This chapter describes the regressions I use to test the three hypotheses.
In every test, I regress post-filing realised volatility on features of the
Item~1A risk-factor section, a common set of controls that are known when the
10-K is filed, and fixed effects.
Section~\ref{sec:outcome} defines the dependent variable and explains when
each variable is measured. Section~\ref{sec:controls} lists the controls. The baseline controls
are the same across the three hypotheses, so the text coefficients can be compared across hypotheses, allowing for the smaller H3 sample. Sections~\ref{sec:h1} and~\ref{sec:h2} cover the joint
test of the volume channel (H1) and the composition channel (H2). I estimate
them together because the density measure for H2 has section length in its
denominator. Section~\ref{sec:h3} introduces the peer-relative omission gap
for H3 and explains how I compare each filing with those of its peers. Section~\ref{sec:estimation}
covers estimation and inference.


% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Outcome variable and timing}
\label{sec:outcome}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The dependent variable is the natural logarithm of annualised post-filing
stock-return volatility measured over a window of $h$~calendar days:
\begin{equation}
\ln \sigma_{i,t,\mathrm{post}}^{[h]} \;=\; \ln\!\left(
\sqrt{252}\cdot
\operatorname{sd}\!\bigl(r_{i,d}\bigr)_{d \in W_{i,t}^{[h]}}
\right),
\label{eq:vol}
\end{equation}
Here $i$ identifies the firm, $t$ the fiscal year of its filing and $h$ the horizon in calendar days. The subscript $\mathrm{post}$ means that returns are measured after this particular filing. For $h=30$, the window covers 30 calendar days and can fall in a different calendar year from fiscal year $t$. Day~0 is the first market trading day on or after the filing date. The first price is taken two market trading days later. The last eligible price is dated at most $h$ calendar days after that start, including the endpoint. $W_{i,t}^{[h]}$ contains the return dates for which both prices lie inside these bounds. For each such date $d$, $r_{i,d}=\ln(P_{i,d}/P_{i,d-1})$, where $P$ is the split- and dividend-adjusted close and $d-1$ denotes the previous available close. The sample standard deviation across these returns measures their daily variability. Multiplication by $\sqrt{252}$ expresses volatility on an annual scale. The 30-day outcome still uses prices from that window alone.

A window needs a minimum number of daily returns
(3 for $h = 5$, 5 for $h = 10$, 14 for $h = 30$, and 44, 89 and 199 for the
longer windows). If there are fewer, or if the standard deviation is zero,
the volatility is treated as missing. A window that would end after the last
trading day in the price data (30~October 2026) is also treated as missing
instead of being cut short. This cut-off applies to the window dates. Missing prices can still make an individual firm's outcome unavailable.

Figure~\ref{fig:timeline} shows the timing. The underlying firm text, accounting figures and price controls in the main models come from the filing or earlier dates. The sample and measurement rules were constructed retrospectively, including the 2025 dictionary and the firm-level tag choices in Chapter~2. The accounting
variables and the text come from the same 10-K, and the price-based controls
use windows that end on the last trading day before the filing date.

\begin{figure}[htbp]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{img/fig_timeline.pdf}
\caption{Timing of the variables for the 10-K of fiscal year $t$ (not to
scale). The outcome window starts two trading days after day~0 and lasts
$h$ calendar days; pre-filing volatility uses the 60 trading days, and the
past return and the liquidity rule the 252 trading days, that end on the
last trading day before the filing date.}
\label{fig:timeline}
\end{figure}

There are two logarithms in this construction. The first converts a price ratio into a daily return. The second applies to the positive volatility estimate used in the regression. I use this second log because the question concerns proportional differences. A move from 20\% to 30\% annualised volatility and a move from 40\% to 60\% both represent a 50\% increase and have the same log difference. The transformation also compresses unusually large values. A volatility below one in decimal units has a negative logarithm even though volatility itself is positive.

At fixed controls, a change $\Delta x$ in a regressor with coefficient $\beta$ corresponds to a relative difference of $100[\exp(\beta\Delta x)-1]\%$ on the back-transformed fitted scale. Doubling the word count gives $\Delta x=\ln 2$. With the joint-model coefficient of 0.053, the implied difference is $100(2^{0.053}-1)\approx3.7\%$. For a density entered as a fraction, a rise of one percentage point gives $\Delta x=0.01$.

The main horizon is $h = 30$ calendar days. I chose it to keep the outcome close to the filing,
so that later news has less time to affect it. Earlier studies that link 10-K
text to later stock-return volatility have used windows of about one year
\parencite{campbell2014, kogan2009, loughran2011}. I report the 365-day window
alongside the main result and use $h \in \{5, 10, 90, 180\}$ as robustness
checks.

\section{Control variables}
\label{sec:controls}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Every regression for H1, H2 and H3 includes the same baseline vector
$\mathbf{X}_{i,t}$ of nine controls. All of them are known at day~0:
\begin{itemize}
  \item pre-filing volatility $\ln\sigma^{\mathrm{pre}}_{i,t}$: the log of the
    annualised standard deviation over a window targeting 60 daily returns (at least 40 returns required), ending on the last
    trading day before the filing date;
  \item firm size $\ln(\text{Market cap})$: the natural log of the product of the as-traded closing price on the
    last trading day before filing and the shares outstanding reported in
    the same 10-K;
  \item book-to-market: book equity attributable to the parent over market
    capitalisation (total equity including NCI where parent equity is not
    reported; Section~\ref{sec:xbrl_tags});
  \item the past 12-month log return, from adjusted prices over the lookback targeting 252 trading-day returns;
  \item leverage (total liabilities over total assets), return on assets
    (consolidated net income over total assets), asset growth (change in assets divided by previous assets), the current
    ratio (current assets divided by current liabilities), and operating cash flow over total assets.
\end{itemize}
Several controls address specific alternative explanations. A firm whose shares were already volatile may describe unsettled conditions at length, and volatility may persist after filing \parencite{kogan2009}. A large, diversified firm may need more words to describe its operations while diversification may dampen fluctuations in its returns. A heavily indebted firm may discuss refinancing at length, while fixed debt obligations make the value of its equity more sensitive to business conditions. Weak operating cash flow can prompt a firm to discuss funding pressures and leave it more exposed to unexpected expenses. Comparing firms at similar prior volatility, size, leverage and operating cash flow helps account for these possibilities. Size, valuation, past returns and leverage also feature in earlier risk-disclosure work \parencite{campbell2014}.

Accounting and valuation ratios enter in levels. This preserves losses, negative book equity and declines in assets where they occur. The trailing return already equals the logarithm of the ratio of the last to the first available adjusted price in the pre-filing window. The model uses that signed return directly. Dictionary shares and the omission gap also enter in levels, so their changes can be expressed in percentage points.

Each firm's first fiscal year is not in the
sample, because asset growth needs the previous year's total assets.
Financial ratios, book-to-market and the past return are winsorised at the
1st and 99th percentiles of each regression sample. Market capitalisation and
pre-filing volatility enter in logs and are not winsorised.

\section{H1 --- Item~1A length and disclosure volume}
\label{sec:h1}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Following \textcite{campbell2014}, I measure disclosure volume as the
logarithm of the Item~1A word count. Campbell et al.\ find that firms with
longer risk-factor sections have higher market-based risk measures later on.
This fits the idea that the amount of risk discussion carries information
about the firm's risk. The volume variable is
\begin{equation}
\text{Words}^{1A}_{i,t} \;=\; \text{number of tokens in Item~1A of firm
$i$'s fiscal-year-$t$ 10-K},
\end{equation}
where a token is a run of at least two letters in the lowercased text
(Section~\ref{ssec:text_postprocessing}). The cleaning removes running
page headers, page numbers and numeric table rows. It keeps the text of
layout tables and footnote markers, so the count is close to the narrative
content but not identical to it. The median Item~1A in the estimation sample
has 8{,}700 tokens (Table~\ref{tab:summary}).

\paragraph{H1.} \emph{If the composition of Item~1A and the controls are held
fixed, a longer Item~1A is associated with higher post-filing volatility}:
$\beta^{\text{len}} > 0$ in
\begin{multline}
\ln \sigma_{i,t,\mathrm{post}}^{[h]} \;=\; \alpha
\;+\; \boldsymbol{\gamma}' \mathbf{X}_{i,t}
\;+\; \beta^{\text{len}} \ln(\text{Words}^{1A}_{i,t}) \\
\;+\; \beta^{\text{dens}} \,\text{RiskDensity}_{i,t}
\;+\; \mu_{j(i,t)} \;+\; \tau_t
\;+\; \varepsilon_{i,t},
\label{eq:joint_model}
\end{multline}
Here $\alpha$ is the intercept. The vector $\mathbf{X}_{i,t}$ contains the nine controls, and $\boldsymbol{\gamma}'\mathbf{X}_{i,t}$ is the sum of each control times its coefficient. The two $\beta$ coefficients are the slopes on log length and risk-word density. The fiscal-year effect $\tau_t$ is shared by all filings for year $t$. The disturbance $\varepsilon_{i,t}$ represents the unexplained component of this filing's log volatility. Each model and horizon has its own coefficients and disturbances.

The index $j(i,t)$ selects the two-digit industry code recorded for firm $i$ at its fiscal-year-$t$ filing. Every filing with that code receives the same industry adjustment $\mu_{j(i,t)}$. The 5{,}782 main-sample observations span 49 industries, equivalent to 48 industry indicators and a reference category when an intercept is included. Nine firms change two-digit industry code during their observed histories, which explains the $t$ in the lookup. The industry and fiscal-year effects enter separately.

Length and density enter together because
length is in the denominator of the density.

\section{H2 --- Risk-word density}
\label{sec:h2}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

H2 is about the composition of Item~1A at a given length. It uses the share of
words from the uncertainty and litigious dictionaries of
\textcite{loughran2011}, which were built for financial reporting:
\begin{equation}
\text{RiskDensity}_{i,t} \;=\; \frac{n^{\text{unc}}_{i,t} + n^{\text{lit}}_{i,t}}
{\text{Words}^{1A}_{i,t}},
\label{eq:risk_density}
\end{equation}
where $n^{\text{unc}}$ and $n^{\text{lit}}$ count the tokens of Item~1A that
belong to each list. The two lists share no word in the dictionary version I
use (Section~\ref{ssec:text_postprocessing}), so no token is counted twice.

Previous studies do not give a clear prediction for how this density measure
relates to volatility, so H2 is two-sided. I consider two possible
explanations.

\textbf{Risk content.} Uncertainty and litigious words may describe uncertain
outcomes and legal risks. \textcite{campbell2014} find that risk-factor
disclosures reflect the risks firms face, and \textcite{kravet2013} find that
increases in risk disclosure go together with increases in stock-return
volatility. If a higher density of risk words means the section says more
about risk, it should be associated with higher volatility.

\textbf{Standard language.} Risk disclosures may also contain general
language that firms repeat or copy. \textcite{cazier2021} show that firms have
reasons to use standard risk language and sometimes borrow it from other
firms. \textcite{dyer2017} document repetition and boilerplate in 10-K
reports, and \textcite{hope2016} find that more specific risk disclosures are
useful to investors and analysts. A high share of risk words may therefore
reflect standard wording and not real information about the firm's risks.
This could weaken the link between risk-word density and volatility, but it
does not by itself predict lower volatility.

\paragraph{H2.} If Item 1A length and the other controls are held fixed,
RiskDensity is associated with post-filing volatility: $(\beta^{\text{dens}}\neq 0)$ in equation~\eqref{eq:joint_model}. The sign shows the direction of the association, but it does not tell us which channel is behind it. Density measures the share of selected words, not how specific the disclosure is, which is what \textcite{hope2016} study. Words such as ``litigation'' or ``investigation'' can also appear in descriptions of very specific events.

\paragraph{Sub-list decomposition.} A second specification replaces
RiskDensity with its two components:
\[
\mathrm{UncDensity}_{i,t} = \frac{n^{\mathrm{unc}}_{i,t}}{\text{Words}^{1A}_{i,t}},
\qquad
\mathrm{LitDensity}_{i,t} = \frac{n^{\mathrm{lit}}_{i,t}}{\text{Words}^{1A}_{i,t}}.
\]
\begin{align}
\ln \sigma_{i,t,\mathrm{post}}^{[h]}
&= \alpha + \boldsymbol{\gamma}'\mathbf{X}_{i,t}
+ \beta^{\text{len}} \ln(\text{Words}^{1A}_{i,t})
\notag \\ &\quad + \beta^{\text{unc}} \mathrm{UncDensity}_{i,t}
+ \beta^{\text{lit}} \mathrm{LitDensity}_{i,t}
+ \mu_{j(i,t)} + \tau_t + \varepsilon_{i,t}.
\label{eq:decomposition_model}
\end{align}
The uncertainty list captures uncertain and imprecise wording. The litigious list captures legal and regulatory language, which does not always point to an actual legal dispute or exposure \parencite{loughran2011}. I also test whether the two coefficients are equal ($\beta^{\text{unc}}=\beta^{\text{lit}}$). This directly compares the estimated associations for a one-percentage-point increase in each word share.

\paragraph{Ratio regressors.} Both densities divide word counts by Item 1A
length. Ratios can give misleading results if the role of the denominator is
not handled carefully \parencite{kronmal1993}. The model includes log length to
account for differences in section length, but this does not guarantee that
the problem goes away. As a robustness check, I replace the densities with
$(\ln(1+n^{\mathrm{unc}}))$ and $(\ln(1+n^{\mathrm{lit}}))$ and keep
$\ln(\text{Words}^{1A})$ (Section~\ref{sec:robustness_h12}). Adding one keeps a zero dictionary count defined on the log scale. This model uses
counts that are not divided by length. Comparing the two sets of results shows
whether the findings depend on how risk language is measured.

\section{H3 --- Peer-relative omission gap}
\label{sec:h3}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

H3 asks whether peer vocabulary absent from a firm's Item~1A is associated with later volatility once length and density are
accounted for. I call this measure the \emph{omission gap}. The measure records differences in words. Their economic meaning is ambiguous. The
firm may simply not face that risk, may use different words for it, or may
write a shorter section for reasons unrelated to risk.

\paragraph{Construction.} For each fiscal year $t$, I fit a term frequency--inverse document frequency (TF--IDF) model on
the Item~1A texts filed before the first fiscal-year-$t$ filing in the panel.
This way, the vocabulary and weights are already known on the date of every
filing I analyse (fiscal year 2010 has no earlier texts, so it has no omission
gap). A word receives more weight if it occurs frequently in a document and is uncommon across the training documents. Appendix~\ref{sec:parser_impl} gives the software settings. Let $\mathbf{x}_{i,t}$ be the vector of word weights for firm $i$'s fiscal-year-$t$ risk section. Peer groups use the fiscal-year-$t$ two-digit SIC code when available and the previous year's code otherwise. For each peer, I use the
most recent Item~1A from fiscal year $t$ or $t-1$ that was filed strictly
before the focal filing. Peer texts are historical, although a peer's year-$t$ industry classification can come from a later filing. With $P_{i,t}$ as
this peer set,
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{p}_{i,t} = \sum_{k \in P_{i,t}} \mathbf{x}^{(t)}_{k},
\qquad
\textsc{Omission}^{1A}_{i,t} \;=\; 1 \;-\;
  \frac{\sum_{w : x_{i,t,w} > 0}\, p_{i,t,w}}{\sum_{w}\, p_{i,t,w}},
\label{eq:omission}
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{x}^{(t)}_k$ represents peer $k$'s selected filing in the focal year's vocabulary. The index $w$ runs over terms in that vocabulary. The component $p_{i,t,w}$ is the pooled peer weight of term $w$, and $x_{i,t,w}$ is its weight in the focal text. The fraction gives the share of peer weight on words present in the focal filing. Subtraction from one gives the missing share. For example, if peer terms carry 100 units of weight in total and terms absent from the filing carry 20, its omission gap is 0.20. It lies between 0 and 1. The gap is missing for fewer than five eligible peers or zero total peer weight.

\paragraph{Length and the sources of variation.} The omission gap is closely
tied to length, because a longer section naturally covers more of the peer
vocabulary. In the H3 sample, its correlation with $\ln(\text{Words}^{1A})$ is
$-0.915$ (Figure~\ref{fig:omission_length}). To give sections a common target length, I draw 20 passages of 3{,}000 consecutive tokens from each longer section and average their omission gaps. For sections of 3{,}000 tokens or fewer, I use the whole section.

\paragraph{Specification.} The H3 regression adds the omission gap to
equation~\eqref{eq:joint_model}:
\begin{multline}
\ln \sigma_{i,t,\mathrm{post}}^{[h]} \;=\; \alpha
\;+\; \boldsymbol{\gamma}' \mathbf{X}_{i,t}
\;+\; \beta^{\text{len}} \ln(\text{Words}^{1A}_{i,t})
\;+\; \beta^{\text{dens}} \,\text{RiskDensity}_{i,t} \\
\;+\; \beta^{\text{om}} \,\textsc{Omission}^{1A}_{i,t}
\;+\; a_{i,t} \;+\; \tau_t
\;+\; \varepsilon_{i,t},
\label{eq:silence_model}
\end{multline}
I estimate it with industry fixed effects ($a_{i,t}=\mu_{j(i,t)}$) and with firm fixed
effects ($a_{i,t}=\mu_i$, one baseline per firm). The controls are the same as in
Section~\ref{sec:controls}, so the H3 model differs from the H1/H2 model
by the omission gap and by the availability of eligible peers. At 30 days, 4{,}659 observations qualify for H3.

H3 predicts that a larger omission gap is associated with higher volatility over the next 30 days. I check whether the full-text and block-based measures agree. Appendix~\ref{sec:app_robust} gives the decision rule and the results at each horizon.

\section{Estimation and inference}
\label{sec:estimation}

I estimate the regressions by ordinary least squares with fixed effects (\texttt{linearmodels}). 
The main model allows each industry to have its own average volatility 
level and each fiscal year to have a common shift. Both persistent differences 
across firms and changes within firms can contribute to its estimates. 
The second model gives each firm its own baseline. Its text coefficient asks 
whether a firm has higher subsequent volatility in years when its disclosure 
is longer than usual, after adjustment for the controls and year effects. 
Both models estimate in-sample associations. An out-of-sample forecast would 
require a separate evaluation.

\paragraph{Fiscal-year effects.} Year fixed effects absorb shocks that hit all
firms in a fiscal year. But fiscal years end in different months, so the
filings for one fiscal year are spread over more than a year of calendar
time (Section~\ref{sec:xbrl_tags}). A fiscal-year effect therefore does not
remove market shocks tied to calendar time. The volatility spike of spring
2020, for example, falls in the post-filing windows of firms with different
fiscal-year labels, and in only part of the windows within a given label.

To account for market conditions around the filing date, I repeat the main regression with a separate effect for each calendar year and month of filing in place of the fiscal-year effects. This compares firms that filed in the same calendar month.

\paragraph{Anticipated volatility.} Item~1A is written when management already
knows current conditions, so expected volatility could shape the wording.
Pre-filing volatility, which is public at filing, captures the part of this
that is visible in prices. It does not rule out that management expects
future volatility beyond that, so I read all results as associations.

\paragraph{Standard errors.} A standard error measures the uncertainty of an estimated coefficient. The test statistic for a zero slope divides that estimate by its standard error. Repeated filings from one firm and firms exposed to the same broad shock can share unexplained movements. Treating their filings as independent could give misleading precision. Standard errors are clustered two-way by firm and
fiscal year \parencite{cameron2011robust, petersen2009}. Firm clustering allows
residuals of the same firm to be correlated across years. Fiscal-year
clustering allows residuals of different firms with the same fiscal-year
label to be correlated. It does not fully capture calendar-time dependence
that crosses fiscal-year labels. The main tables use large-sample normal-reference $p$-values. The coefficient plots show the estimate plus or minus 1.96 standard errors. For example, the 30-day joint-model length coefficient is 0.05331 with a two-way standard error of 0.01181, giving a test statistic of 4.51.

The sample spans only 15 fiscal years, so the usual tests may overstate precision \parencite{cameron2008}. As a second check on H1 and H2 at 30 days, I use a wild cluster bootstrap by year. Starting with a model that sets the text coefficient to zero, I repeatedly give each fiscal year a random weight on its residuals and recalculate the test statistic. The bootstrap reports how often the result is at least as extreme as the one observed. All filings in the same fiscal year receive the same weight. The main standard errors also allow for links between filings by the same firm across years. Table~\ref{tab:inference} reports both sets of results.

The text variables enter without winsorisation. Table~\ref{tab:summary} 
reports their distributions.

\paragraph{Sample.} The 30-day estimation sample has 5{,}782 firm-years from
512 firms; the same firm-years enter the 365-day models.
Table~\ref{tab:attrition} in Appendix~\ref{app:variables} shows how the sample
is built, from the SEC ticker list down to this sample.

The sample was selected using the October 2026 SEC list. 
This limits inference about firms that left the market during the study period 
(Section~\ref{sec:sample_selection}).
