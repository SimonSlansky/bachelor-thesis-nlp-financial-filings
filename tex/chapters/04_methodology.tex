% =============================================================================
\chapter{Methodology}
\label{ch:methodology}
% =============================================================================

This chapter sets out the regression specifications for the three
hypotheses. All tests use a dynamic panel regression in which
post-filing realised volatility depends on its own lag, financial
controls, and one or more characteristics of the Item~1A
risk-factor section, with industry and fiscal-year fixed effects.
Section~\ref{sec:outcome} defines the dependent variable;
Section~\ref{sec:controls} lists the controls, which are the same in
all specifications so that the text coefficients can be compared.
Sections~\ref{sec:h1} and~\ref{sec:h2} describe the joint test of
the volume channel (H1) and the specificity channel (H2), which must
be estimated together because the density measure for H2 has
section length in its denominator. Section~\ref{sec:h3} introduces
the peer-relative omission gap for H3, and the rest of the chapter
covers estimation, inference, and robustness checks.


% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────
\section{Outcome variable: post-filing realised volatility}
\label{sec:outcome}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

The dependent variable is the natural logarithm of annualised
post-filing stock-return volatility measured over a window of
$h$~calendar days:
\begin{equation}
\ln \sigma_{i,t+1}^{[h]} \;=\; \ln\!\left(
\sqrt{252}\cdot
\operatorname{sd}\!\bigl(r_{i,d}\bigr)_{d \in W_{i,t}^{[h]}}
\right),
\label{eq:vol}
\end{equation}
where
\begin{equation*}
r_{i,d} \;=\; \ln\!\left(\frac{P_{i,d}}{P_{i,d-1}}\right)
\end{equation*}
is the daily log return for firm~$i$, and $W_{i,t}^{[h]}$ denotes
the post-filing window associated with filing year~$t$. Day~0 is
the first trading day on or after the 10-K filing date. The window
begins two trading days after day~0, so the market reaction on the
filing day and the next day is left out, and it then runs for
$h$~calendar days. Only returns between two prices inside the
window are used. A window needs a minimum number of daily returns
(3 for $h = 5$, 5 for $h = 10$, 14 for $h = 30$, about half the
trading days for longer windows); if it has fewer, or if all prices
in it are the same, the volatility is treated as missing. The daily standard
deviation is annualised by multiplying by $\sqrt{252}$; this is a
constant rescaling and does not affect inference.

We use the natural logarithm of volatility, so a coefficient $\beta$
implies a change of $100(e^{\beta}-1)\%$ in volatility,
approximately $100\beta\%$ for small $\beta$. The main
specification uses a 30-day post-filing horizon ($h = 30$);
horizons of $h \in \{5, 10, 90, 180, 365\}$ are reported as
exploratory checks.

\section{Control variables}
\label{sec:controls}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Every regression includes the same vector
$\mathbf{X}^{\text{fin}}_{i,t}$ of firm-level controls drawn from
the annual XBRL filings (Section~\ref{sec:xbrl_tags}): firm size
$\ln(\text{Total Assets})$, leverage (total liabilities over total
assets), return on assets (net income over total assets), and
asset growth (year-on-year change in total assets divided by
lagged total assets). Lagged log-volatility
$\ln \sigma_{i,t}^{[h]}$ enters with its own coefficient $\phi$.
It uses the window of the same length~$h$ after the firm's
filing for the \emph{previous} fiscal year ($t-1$). Each firm's
first fiscal year is not in the regression sample, because asset
growth needs the previous year's total assets; that first filing is
still used to compute the lagged volatility of the second year.
If the firm has
no filing for year $t-1$, the lag is missing; we never use an older
year instead. The lagged window is also cut off on the day before
the current filing, so it never overlaps the outcome window. This
matters only for $h = 365$~days, where a full year after the
previous filing would otherwise run past the current filing date.
All ratios are winsorised at the 1st and 99th
percentiles of the regression sample. The H3 regression replaces
asset growth with two liquidity proxies (current ratio and
operating cash flow over assets). The reason is given in
Section~\ref{sec:h3}.

\section{H1 --- Item~1A length and disclosure volume}
\label{sec:h1}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

Following \textcite{campbell2014}, disclosure volume is measured as
the logarithm of the Item~1A word count. Campbell et al.\ show that
firms with longer risk-factor disclosures later have higher
market-based risk measures, including return volatility, consistent
with disclosure volume carrying information about firm risk. We
predict a positive association between Item~1A length and
post-filing realised volatility. The volume variable is
\begin{equation}
\text{Words}^{1A}_{i,t} \;=\; \text{number of tokens in Item~1A
of firm~$i$'s year-$t$ 10-K},
\end{equation}
and $\ln(\text{Words}^{1A}_{i,t})$ enters the regression. The
text-cleaning pipeline (Section~\ref{ssec:text_postprocessing})
removes XBRL tags, footnote markers, and table cells before
tokenisation, so the count reflects narrative content only. The
median Item~1A holds about $8{,}800$~words, with a long right tail
(Section~\ref{sec:descriptive}).

The volume channel is identified jointly with the specificity
channel of H2 in a single regression (the
\emph{joint specification}):
\begin{multline}
\ln \sigma_{i,t+1}^{[h]} \;=\; \alpha
\;+\; \phi \, \ln \sigma_{i,t}^{[h]}
\;+\; \boldsymbol{\beta}' \mathbf{X}^{\text{fin}}_{i,t}
\;+\; \beta^{\text{len}} \cdot \ln(\text{Words}^{1A}_{i,t}) \\
\;+\; \beta^{\text{dens}} \cdot \text{RiskDensity}_{i,t}
\;+\; \mu_j \;+\; \tau_t
\;+\; \varepsilon_{i,t},
\label{eq:joint_model}
\end{multline}
where $\mu_j$ is a two-digit SIC industry fixed effect, $\tau_t$
is a fiscal-year fixed effect, and $\text{RiskDensity}$ is defined
in Section~\ref{sec:h2}. H1 predicts $\beta^{\text{len}} > 0$.
Length and density must enter together because the dictionary
risk-density used in prior work has length in its denominator.

\section{H2 --- Risk-word density and disclosure specificity}
\label{sec:h2}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

H2 is motivated by the disclosure-specificity framework of
\textcite{hope2016}. Hope et al.\ argue that risk disclosures are
more informative when they contain firm-specific details rather than
generic statements that could apply to any firm. They find that
more specific risk-factor disclosures lead to stronger market
reactions and better analyst assessments of firm risk.

We do not measure specificity directly. Instead, we test whether
the word composition of Item~1A is associated with future
volatility after controlling for length and the usual financial
determinants of volatility. We focus on the share of words from the
uncertainty and litigious dictionaries of \textcite{loughran2011},
which were built for financial reporting.

Two disclosures of the same length may convey information
differently. One may use more standard risk vocabulary; the other
may use more firm-specific narrative. A higher concentration of
dictionary risk words may therefore mean that more of the
disclosure is generic risk framing rather than discussion of
firm-specific exposures. This interpretation needs care, because
words such as ``litigation'', ``regulatory'', ``investigation'', or
``compliance'' can describe very firm-specific circumstances.
Risk-word density is thus an indirect proxy for the balance between
standard risk terminology and other narrative, not a direct measure
of specificity in the sense of \textcite{hope2016}.

Under this interpretation, a section with less standard risk
vocabulary leaves more room for firm-specific discussion of risks,
operations, counterparties, products, and events. If firm-specific
disclosure helps investors understand risk \parencite{hope2016},
higher risk-word density should be associated with lower subsequent
volatility, holding length fixed. H2 predicts a negative relation.
The test has two stages: the first uses a composite risk-word
density, and the second splits it into the two
\textcite{loughran2011} sub-lists.

\subsection{Composite RiskDensity}
\label{ssec:h2_composite}

The first-stage variable is the share of Item~1A words that match
either the Loughran--McDonald uncertainty or litigious dictionaries:
\begin{equation}
\text{RiskDensity}_{i,t} \;=\; \frac{
n^{\text{unc}}_{i,t} + n^{\text{lit}}_{i,t}
}{
\text{Words}^{1A}_{i,t}
},
\label{eq:risk_density}
\end{equation}
where $n^{\text{unc}}$ and $n^{\text{lit}}$ denote the numbers of
uncertainty- and litigious-dictionary words appearing in firm~$i$'s
Item~1A disclosure and $\text{Words}^{1A}$ is the total number of
words in that section.

Higher values mean that more of the section consists of dictionary
risk words. As discussed above, RiskDensity is not a direct measure
of specificity; it measures how prominent standard risk language is
in the section. Lower values mean that more of the text falls
outside the risk lexicon and may contain more firm-specific
information, though not necessarily in every case.

H2 is tested through the coefficient $\beta^{\text{dens}}$ in
equation~\eqref{eq:joint_model}, with the prediction
\begin{equation*}
\beta^{\text{dens}} < 0.
\end{equation*}

\subsection{LM sub-list decomposition (second stage)}
\label{ssec:h2_decomp}

RiskDensity combines two different kinds of risk language. To see
which one drives any relation, the second stage splits RiskDensity
into two sub-densities:
\begin{align}
\text{UncDensity}_{i,t} &\;=\; \frac{n^{\text{unc}}_{i,t}}
  {\text{Words}^{1A}_{i,t}},
\label{eq:unc_density}\\[4pt]
\text{LitDensity}_{i,t} &\;=\; \frac{n^{\text{lit}}_{i,t}}
  {\text{Words}^{1A}_{i,t}},
\label{eq:lit_density}
\end{align}
such that
\begin{equation*}
\text{RiskDensity}_{i,t} \;=\; \text{UncDensity}_{i,t}
  + \text{LitDensity}_{i,t}.
\end{equation*}
The uncertainty dictionary covers language about ambiguity and
incomplete knowledge of future outcomes. The litigious dictionary
covers legal exposure, regulation, compliance, investigations,
claims, and disputes. Both are risk-related, but they measure
different things \parencite{loughran2011}.

The second-stage specification replaces the composite measure with
its two components:
\begin{multline}
\ln \sigma_{i,t+1}^{[h]} \;=\; \alpha
\;+\; \phi \, \ln \sigma_{i,t}^{[h]}
\;+\; \boldsymbol{\beta}' \mathbf{X}^{\text{fin}}_{i,t}
\;+\; \beta^{\text{len}} \cdot \ln(\text{Words}^{1A}_{i,t}) \\
\;+\; \beta^{\text{unc}} \cdot \text{UncDensity}_{i,t}
\;+\; \beta^{\text{lit}} \cdot \text{LitDensity}_{i,t}
\;+\; \mu_j \;+\; \tau_t
\;+\; \varepsilon_{i,t}.
\label{eq:decomposition_model}
\end{multline}
The two coefficients are separately identified because the sample
correlation between UncDensity and LitDensity is close to zero
($r \approx -0.05$). If the negative association is concentrated
in LitDensity while the UncDensity coefficient is small and
insignificant, the relation comes mainly from legal and regulatory
terminology rather than general uncertainty language.

\section{H3 --- Peer-relative omission gap}
\label{sec:h3}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

H3 asks whether what a firm \emph{leaves out} of Item~1A relative
to its industry peers carries information about future volatility,
beyond what length and density already capture. Throughout the
thesis the \emph{omission gap} is the measure (the share of peer
vocabulary a firm leaves out), and the \emph{strategic-silence
hypothesis} is the interpretation tested by H3: that a larger
omission gap reflects a firm staying quiet on risks its peers
disclose.

\paragraph{Construction.}
For each fiscal year $t$ we build a TF--IDF representation of every
Item~1A in the panel, keeping terms that appear in at least five
filings and at most $95\%$ of filings, with $\ell_2$-normalised rows. Let
$\mathbf{x}_{i,t}$ be the TF--IDF row for firm~$i$ in year~$t$.
For each two-digit SIC industry $j$ in year~$t$ with at least six
panel filings, the leave-one-out peer aggregate is
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{p}_{i,t} \;=\; \sum_{k \in S_{j(i),t}, k \neq i}
  \mathbf{x}_{k,t},
\label{eq:peer_aggregate}
\end{equation}
where $S_{j,t}$ is the set of panel firms in SIC2 industry $j$ that
filed in year~$t$. The peer aggregate summarises what the focal
firm's industry peers chose to write that year. The omission gap
is the share of peer disclosure mass on terms the focal firm does
not mention:
\begin{equation}
\textsc{Omission}^{1A}_{i,t} \;=\; 1 \;-\;
  \frac{\sum_{w : x_{i,t,w} > 0}\, p_{i,t,w}}
       {\sum_{w}\, p_{i,t,w}},
\label{eq:omission}
\end{equation}
with values in $[0, 1]$. Zero means the focal firm covers every
term any peer mentions. Values near one mean the firm covers only
a small share of what its peers chose to discuss. Firm-years in
SIC2-year cells with fewer than five peers are dropped because the
peer aggregate is too noisy.

\paragraph{Why SIC2 and why control for length.}
SIC2 is the standard peer benchmark in the disclosure-similarity
literature \parencite{brown2011, dyer2017}. The median peer cell
has 35~firms (P25~=~14). The omission gap is also negatively
correlated with Item~1A length ($r = -0.85$), since a longer
section mechanically covers more peer terms. The H3 regression
controls for $\ln(\text{Words}^{1A})$, so the omission coefficient
holds length fixed. The H3 control vector
also replaces asset growth with two liquidity proxies, the current
ratio and operating cash flow over assets.\footnote{The swap follows
prior risk-disclosure studies that include liquidity-related controls
\parencite{kravet2013, dyer2017}.
Section~\ref{sec:robustness_silence} re-estimates
equation~\ref{eq:silence_model} with the baseline H1+H2 control
vector to confirm that the H3 result is not driven by the choice
of controls.}

\paragraph{Specification.}
The H3 regression adds the omission gap to the joint
specification:
\begin{multline}
\ln \sigma_{i,t+1}^{[h]} \;=\; \alpha
\;+\; \phi \, \ln \sigma_{i,t}^{[h]}
\;+\; \boldsymbol{\beta}' \mathbf{X}^{\text{fin}}_{i,t}
\;+\; \beta^{\text{len}} \cdot \ln(\text{Words}^{1A}_{i,t}) \\
\;+\; \beta^{\text{dens}} \cdot \text{RiskDensity}_{i,t}
\;+\; \beta^{\text{om}} \cdot \textsc{Omission}^{1A}_{i,t}
\;+\; \mu_j \;+\; \tau_t
\;+\; \varepsilon_{i,t}.
\label{eq:silence_model}
\end{multline}
Equation~\ref{eq:silence_model} is run under two fixed-effect
structures: the cross-sectional version with $\mu_j$ a SIC2
industry fixed effect, and a stricter within-firm version in which
$\mu_j$ is replaced by a firm fixed effect $\mu_i$. The within-firm
version is more demanding because it uses only year-to-year
variation in omission driven by changes in peer disclosure.
H3 is \emph{fully} supported if $\beta^{\text{om}} > 0$ at the
headline $h = 30$~days under both structures. If the sign is
positive and significant only under the stricter firm-FE
structure, H3 receives \emph{partial} support: the
strategic-silence channel is then identified within firms but not
as a between-firm regularity. Horizons run over
$h \in \{5, 10, 30, 90, 180\}$~days. $h = 365$~days is dropped
because the post-filing window for fiscal years $2023$--$2024$
extends past the data cut-off.

The H3 sample has $4{,}857$ to $4{,}871$~firm-years at
$h \geq 10$~days, smaller than the H1+H2 sample of $6{,}048$ at
$h = 30$~days. There are two reasons: the
$\geq 5$~peers-per-cell restriction, and the two liquidity
controls, which are missing for some firm-years. At $h = 5$~days
the sample falls to $4{,}226$, because many 5-day windows do not
contain enough daily returns.

\section{Estimation and inference}
\label{sec:estimation}
% ─────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────────

All regressions are estimated by ordinary least squares with the
relevant fixed effects absorbed using the \texttt{linearmodels}
package. Coefficients are identified from
\emph{within-industry, within-year} (or within-firm, within-year)
variation, which removes common time trends in volatility (such as
the 2008 and 2020 crises) and persistent industry- or firm-level
differences in risk vocabulary. The analysis is predictive, not
causal: the coefficients measure the conditional association
between Item~1A disclosure and \emph{subsequent} realised
volatility, as in \textcite{campbell2014}, not the effect of
disclosure on volatility.

\paragraph{Limitation: anticipated volatility.}
Item~1A is written when management already knows current
volatility conditions, so expected volatility could shape the
wording (reverse causality). The design addresses this only
partly: the outcome window starts two trading days after filing,
lagged log-volatility is controlled for, fixed effects absorb
persistent firm and industry differences, and the lead-lag check
in Section~\ref{sec:robustness_silence} tests the timing. It does
not rule out that management anticipates \emph{future}
volatility not captured by lagged volatility, so we interpret the
results as predictive associations.

\paragraph{Primary horizon.}
The headline horizon is $h = 30$~days, at which H1--H3 are
tested. The other horizons in
Table~\ref{tab:horizons} are reported as exploratory evidence on
the time decay of the signal.

\paragraph{Standard errors.}
Standard errors are two-way clustered by firm and fiscal year
\parencite{petersen2009, cameron2011robust}, because (i)~consecutive
firm-year volatility windows overlap, which creates within-firm
serial correlation, and (ii)~calendar-year shocks (above all COVID
in 2020) create within-year cross-sectional correlation that
one-way firm clustering would miss.

\paragraph{Outliers.}
Financial ratios are winsorised at the 1st and 99th percentiles of
the regression sample. The text variables are not winsorised:
$\ln(\text{Words}^{1A})$ is bounded by the log transform and
$\text{RiskDensity}$ is a ratio with sample maximum
$\approx 0.05$.

\paragraph{Sample.}
The estimation sample at $h = 30$~days has $6{,}048$~firm-years on
$526$~unique firms; the $h = 365$~days sample has
$6{,}038$~firm-years on the same firms. The 5-day sample is
smaller ($5{,}234$~firm-years), because many 5-day windows do not
contain enough daily returns.

\paragraph{Limitation: survivorship.}
The list of firms comes from the SEC company list as it is today,
so it only contains firms that still exist. Firms that were
delisted, acquired, or went bankrupt during 2010--2024 are missing,
and the 90\% return-coverage rule (Section~\ref{sec:sample_selection})
removes a few more. The panel grows from 149~firms in fiscal year
2010 to over 500 in 2020--2024, which shows this. Missing firms are
likely to be riskier, so the results describe large firms that
survived, and they may not hold for firms in distress.

\paragraph{Stationarity.}
Because the specification is dynamic---lagged log-volatility enters
with its own coefficient $\phi$---an integrated regressor could
produce a spurious association. We test every regression variable
for a unit root with the augmented Dickey--Fuller test. The
results, in Section~\ref{sec:robustness_stationarity}, indicate
that the panel is stationary, $I(0)$.
