\chapter*{Introduction}
\addcontentsline{toc}{chapter}{Introduction}
\label{ch:introduction}

The Item~1A risk-factor section of the U.S.\ 10-K, mandatory since
2005, is the longest and most carefully written piece of
risk-disclosure language in U.S.\ corporate reporting. Two parallel
literatures argue that this section conveys economically meaningful
information about firm-level risk, but they reach opposite
predictions about its sign. \textcite{campbell2014} show that
\emph{larger} risk-factor sections, both in raw word count and in
the count of risk-related words, are associated with higher
subsequent return volatility, market beta, and bid--ask spreads:
when managers write more about risk, they are signalling more risk.
\textcite{hope2016} show that, for a given length, risk-factor
sections that contain a higher proportion of \emph{specific}
content---names, numbers, geographies---are associated with
stronger analyst-forecast revisions and lower implied cost of
capital: more concrete risk language pins down exposures and reduces
uncertainty.

Much of the empirical literature operationalises both predictions
through a single statistic, the dictionary-based risk-word density of
Item~1A: the share of the section's words that match the
\textcite{loughran2011} uncertainty and litigious word lists. This
ratio, however, has section length in its denominator. The volume
channel (numerator) and the specificity channel (denominator,
inverted) are therefore mixed into one number that has been hard to
sign cleanly in the data.

This thesis makes a small methodological move with substantive
payoff: it decomposes the dictionary risk-density into its two
components---the natural log of the Item~1A word count, and the
risk-word density itself---and enters them jointly in a panel
regression of post-filing log-volatility. With both regressors in
the specification, length captures the volume channel net of any
change in boilerplate share, and density captures the specificity
channel net of any change in volume. Both predictions can therefore
be tested in the same equation, and the data can adjudicate.

On an unbalanced panel of 535 large U.S.\ non-financial firms over
fiscal years 2010--2024 the joint specification at the 30-day
horizon delivers exactly the two predictions: longer Item~1A
sections predict higher post-filing volatility (the volume effect,
H1), while denser Item~1A sections, holding length constant, predict
lower volatility (the specificity effect, H2). Both signs and
significance survive at every post-filing horizon between 5 and 365
days and across pre-COVID and COVID-era sub-samples; the joint
specification also reveals that the dictionary-only risk-density
estimate of the specificity channel is roughly twice as large as the
true effect, because the omitted volume channel loads onto it
through the negative cross-sectional correlation between length and
density.

A third contribution looks inside the LM risk lexicon itself.
Decomposing the composite RiskDensity into its two LM
sub-components---uncertainty-list and litigious-list densities,
each normalised by Item~1A length---reveals that the entire
negative specificity effect is carried by the LM-Litigious
sub-list (H3): conditional on length, LitDensity has a coefficient
of $-6.16$ ($t = -3.35$) on 30-day post-filing log-volatility,
while UncDensity is small and statistically indistinguishable from
zero. The pattern survives at every horizon and on both COVID
sub-samples. The interpretation aligns with the
\textcite{nelson2007,cazier2021,beatty2019} reading of
litigious-list language as standardised cautionary boilerplate
produced under the safe-harbour regime of the 1995 Private
Securities Litigation Reform Act and characteristic of mature,
legally supervised firm-level disclosure environments.

The remainder of the thesis is organised as follows.
Chapter~\ref{ch:literature} reviews the volume, specificity, and
boilerplate literatures and motivates the three hypotheses.
Chapter~\ref{ch:data} describes the SEC EDGAR data, the textual
extraction pipeline, and the construction of the financial controls
and the post-filing volatility windows. Chapter~\ref{ch:methodology}
formalises the joint specification and the LM-list decomposition.
Chapter~\ref{ch:results} reports the headline tests of H1, H2, and
H3. Chapter~\ref{ch:robustness} reports the multi-horizon evidence,
pre/post-COVID splits, a within-firm fixed-effects specification,
and a transparent account of seven alternative H3 candidates that
were tested and rejected. The Conclusion summarises the contribution
and discusses limitations and suggested extensions.
